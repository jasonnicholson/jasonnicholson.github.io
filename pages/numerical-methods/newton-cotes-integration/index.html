Newton-Cotes Integration

← Numerical Methods

Source inspiration: (Mathew 2000-2019).

Description

Newton-Cotes formulas approximate \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) from values of \(f\) at equally spaced nodes. On a panel of \(M\) subintervals of width \(h\), with nodes \(x_k=x_0+kh\) and \(f_k=f(x_k)\), they integrate the polynomial of degree \(M\) through the \(M+1\) nodes; the closed rules include both endpoints. Mathews states the first four with their error terms, for some \(c\) in the panel:

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_{x_0}^{x_1} f(x)\,dx &= \frac{h}{2}\left(f_0+f_1\right) - \frac{h^3}{12}f^{(2)}(c) &&\text{(trapezoidal)}\\ \int_{x_0}^{x_2} f(x)\,dx &= \frac{h}{3}\left(f_0+4f_1+f_2\right) - \frac{h^5}{90}f^{(4)}(c) &&\text{(Simpson's 1/3)}\\ \int_{x_0}^{x_3} f(x)\,dx &= \frac{3h}{8}\left(f_0+3f_1+3f_2+f_3\right) - \frac{3h^5}{80}f^{(4)}(c) &&\text{(Simpson's 3/8)}\\ \int_{x_0}^{x_4} f(x)\,dx &= \frac{2h}{45}\left(7f_0+32f_1+12f_2+32f_3+7f_4\right) - \frac{8h^7}{945}f^{(6)}(c) &&\text{(Boole)} \end{aligned} \]

A composite rule splits \([a,b]\) into \(n\) subintervals, \(h=(b-a)/n\), and applies the panel rule to each of \(n/M\) panels, so \(n\) must be a multiple of \(M\); Cases 1 to 4 use \(M=1\), 2, 3 and 4.

Each error term needs \(f\) smooth on the panel: \(f\in C^2\) for the trapezoidal rule, \(C^4\) for both Simpson rules and \(C^6\) for Boole’s rule. The rules are exact for polynomials of degree 1, 3, 3 and 5, their degrees of precision; for even \(M\) the symmetric panel gains one degree. In general, if \(f\) has those continuous derivatives on \([a,b]\), the composite errors are \(O(h^2)\), \(O(h^4)\), \(O(h^4)\) and \(O(h^6)\).

The animations do not show these orders: every case uses \(f(x)=e^{-x}\sin(8x^{2/3})+1\) on \([0,2]\), which is not smooth at \(x=0\). The \(x^{2/3}\) term makes \(f'\) unbounded at \(x=0\) (\(f'(x)\approx \tfrac{16}{3}x^{-1/3}\) near \(0\)), so the textbook error order, which assumes smooth \(f\), does not hold for this integrand. The measured error of all four rules shrinks like \(O(h^{5/3})\) rather than \(O(h^2)\), \(O(h^4)\), \(O(h^4)\) and \(O(h^6)\): halving \(h\) (from 24 to 48 subintervals) divides it by about 3.14, 3.38, 3.55 and 3.15 for the trapezoidal, Simpson’s \(1/3\), Simpson’s \(3/8\) and Boole’s rules, tending to \(2^{5/3}\approx 3.17\) for each, instead of 4, 16, 16 and 64. So at equal \(h\) the higher-degree rules gain only a constant factor over the trapezoidal rule here, not extra powers of \(h\). At 121 sample points the panel at \(x=0\) contributes over 99% of the error of Simpson’s \(3/8\) and Boole’s rules (Case 3, Case 4). Adaptive Simpson’s Rule targets such errors by refining only panels whose error estimate is large. Raising \(M\) is no cure either: the closed rule on nine nodes (\(M=8\)) already has negative weights, which can amplify rounding errors in the \(f_k\).

Each rule has its own page (Trapezoidal, Simpson’s, Simpson’s 3/8, Boole’s); the panel polynomials are Lagrange interpolants, Romberg Integration recovers Simpson’s and Boole’s rules from trapezoidal sums, and Gauss-Legendre Quadrature drops equal spacing for degree of precision \(2N-1\) with \(N\) nodes.

Animations

Each case uses \(f(x)=e^{-x}\sin(8x^{2/3})+1\) on \([0,2]\) and shows a different closed Newton-Cotes construction while sample density increases frame by frame.

Case 1 — Composite Trapezoidal Rule

Behavior: Linear segments connect adjacent sample points, creating trapezoids that refine as nodes get denser.

Case 2 — Composite Simpson’s Rule (1/3)

Behavior: Pairs of subintervals are approximated by quadratics, producing a visibly smoother top boundary than trapezoids. This animation stops at 41 sample points, as Mathews’ Simpson’s rule animation does, so its last frame is less accurate than the 121-point last frames of the other cases; at 41 points its error (\(2.4\times10^{-3}\)) is smaller than the trapezoidal rule’s (\(8.3\times10^{-3}\)).

Julia source

Case 3 — Composite Simpson’s 3/8 Rule

Behavior: Triplets of subintervals are approximated by cubics. This is a higher-degree piecewise fit than Simpson’s \(1/3\) rule, but not a higher-order rule: both have degree of precision 3 (see Derivation Notes). On this integrand its error at the same \(h\) is larger, about 1.25 times Simpson’s \(1/3\) error as \(h\) shrinks.

Julia source

Case 4 — Composite Boole’s Rule

Behavior: Blocks of four subintervals use quartic interpolation. As density rises, the piecewise quartic envelope tracks oscillations closely.

Julia source

Legacy note: each case uses the same rule, integrand and interval as Mathews’ animation of that rule, and at every number of sample points the two share, the approximations agree to six decimals.

Derivation Notes

The four closed Newton-Cotes rules fit interpolants of degree 1, 2, 3 and 4 per panel, but their degrees of precision are 1, 3, 3 and 5: the rules with an even number of subintervals per panel (Simpson’s \(1/3\), Boole) are exact for one degree more than their interpolant. For smooth \(f\) (\(C^2\), \(C^4\), \(C^4\) and \(C^6\) on \([a,b]\) respectively) the composite errors are therefore \(O(h^2)\), \(O(h^4)\), \(O(h^4)\) and \(O(h^6)\). Simpson’s \(3/8\) rule is thus not higher order than Simpson’s \(1/3\) rule, and its composite error constant is larger: \(|E|=\tfrac{1}{80}(b-a)h^4|f^{(4)}(\xi)|\) against \(\tfrac{1}{180}(b-a)h^4|f^{(4)}(\xi)|\) for some \(\xi\in[a,b]\). Rules of very high degree on wide panels can become less robust.
