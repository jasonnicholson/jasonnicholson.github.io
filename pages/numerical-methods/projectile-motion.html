Projectile Motion

← Numerical Methods

Source inspiration: (Mathew 2000-2019).

Description

A projectile moves under gravity and, in a real medium, under air resistance that opposes its velocity. Without air resistance the equations of motion can be integrated by hand and the path is a parabola. With air resistance proportional to the velocity there is still a closed-form solution, but with resistance proportional to the square or the \(\tfrac32\) power of the speed the formulas become awkward or unavailable, and a numerical solution is the practical route. Each model is a second-order differential equation, or a pair of them, for the position.

The method is the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method (RK4), applied to the first-order system obtained by introducing the velocity as a second unknown. For one second-order equation \(y'' = f(t, y, y')\) with \(y(a) = \alpha\), \(y'(a) = \beta\), write \(\mathbf{Z} = (y, v)\) with \(v = y'\) and \(\mathbf{F}(t, \mathbf{Z}) = \bigl(v,\ f(t, y, v)\bigr)\), and step with \(h = (b - a)/n\):

\[ \mathbf{Z}_{j+1} = \mathbf{Z}_j + \tfrac16\left(\mathbf{k}_1 + 2\mathbf{k}_2 + 2\mathbf{k}_3 + \mathbf{k}_4\right), \qquad \begin{aligned} \mathbf{k}_1 &= h\,\mathbf{F}(t_j, \mathbf{Z}_j), & \mathbf{k}_2 &= h\,\mathbf{F}\bigl(t_j + \tfrac h2, \mathbf{Z}_j + \tfrac12\mathbf{k}_1\bigr),\\ \mathbf{k}_3 &= h\,\mathbf{F}\bigl(t_j + \tfrac h2, \mathbf{Z}_j + \tfrac12\mathbf{k}_2\bigr), & \mathbf{k}_4 &= h\,\mathbf{F}(t_j + h, \mathbf{Z}_j + \mathbf{k}_3). \end{aligned} \]

Mathews’ module has two parts. In the first (Examples 1–4) an arrow is shot straight up at \(300\) ft/s and the four drag laws are compared through the ascent time, the maximum height, the descent time and the impact velocity. In the second (Examples 5–8) the same method, applied to two coupled second-order equations for \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\), traces the flight of a long-hit baseball. Throughout, \(g = 32\) ft/s² and lengths are in feet.

Motion without air resistance

If a projectile leaves the origin with speed \(v_0\) at angle \(\theta\) to the horizontal and only gravity acts, its position is

\[ x(t) = v_0 \cos(\theta)\, t, \tag{1} \]

\[ y(t) = v_0 \sin(\theta)\, t - \tfrac12 g t^2. \tag{2} \]

Solving (1) for \(t = \dfrac{\sec(\theta)}{v_0}\, x\) and substituting in (2) gives the parabola

\[ y = \tan(\theta)\, x - \frac{g \sec^2(\theta)}{2 v_0^2}\, x^2 . \]

The highest point is reached when \(y'(t) = v_0 \sin(\theta) - g t = 0\), at time \(t_1\), and the projectile is back at height \(0\) at time \(t_2\):

\[ t_1 = \frac{v_0 \sin(\theta)}{g}, \qquad y_{\max} = y(t_1) = \frac{v_0^2 \sin^2(\theta)}{2g}, \qquad t_2 = \frac{2 v_0 \sin(\theta)}{g} = 2 t_1 . \]

So without air resistance the descent takes exactly as long as the ascent. The range is

\[ x_{\text{range}} = x(t_2) = \frac{v_0^2}{g}\,\sin(2\theta). \]

TipTheorem — Angle of maximum range

For a fixed launch speed \(v_0\), no air resistance, and launch and landing at the same height, the range \(x_{\text{range}} = \frac{v_0^2}{g}\sin(2\theta)\) is largest when \(\theta = \pi/4\). The conditions matter: with drag, or with a launch point above the landing level, the best angle differs from \(45^\circ\). For a ball hit at \(127\) ft/s from \(3\) ft above the ground (Example 5 below) the longest carry comes at \(\theta = 44.83^\circ\), and at \(207\) ft/s with the linear drag of Example 6 at \(\theta = 31.8^\circ\).

For example, with \(v_0 = 300\) ft/s the range is \(2812.5\) ft at \(\theta = 45^\circ\) and \(2435.70\) ft at both \(30^\circ\) and \(60^\circ\).

Vertical motion with air resistance

Now fire the projectile straight up, so \(x(t) = 0\), and let \(y(t)\) be its height and \(v = y'\) its velocity. The drag force \(F_R\) always opposes the motion. A very slow object in a viscous fluid feels a drag proportional to its speed; faster objects (and Millikan’s oil drops, as Mathews notes) are better described by a drag proportional to the square of the speed. The Reynolds number decides which regime applies. Mathews compares four laws.

NoteDefinition — Vertical drag models (Projectile Motion I)

With mass \(m\) and drag constant \(k > 0\):

  1. No air resistance: \(\; m \dfrac{dv}{dt} = -mg\), \(\quad y'' = -g\).

  2. Drag proportional to the velocity: \(\; m \dfrac{dv}{dt} = -kv - mg\), \(\quad y'' = -\dfrac{k}{m}\, y' - g\).

  3. Drag proportional to the square of the velocity: \(\; m \dfrac{dv}{dt} = -kv^2 - mg\) on the ascent (\(v > 0\)) and \(m \dfrac{dv}{dt} = kv^2 - mg\) on the descent (\(v < 0\)); both are the single equation \(\; y'' = -\dfrac{k}{m}\, y' \lvert y' \rvert - g\).

  4. Drag proportional to the \(\tfrac32\) power of the speed: \(\; y'' = -\dfrac{k}{m}\, y' \sqrt{\lvert y' \rvert} - g\).

The factor \(y'\lvert y'\rvert\) in (iii) is what makes the drag point the right way: it is \(+v^2\) going up and \(-v^2\) coming down. Writing \(-k v^2\) for both halves of the flight is a mistake that Example 3 examines.

When the drag grows with the speed, a falling object approaches a terminal velocity at which drag balances gravity: \(-mg/k\) for law (ii), \(-\sqrt{mg/k}\) for law (iii), and \(-(mg/k)^{2/3}\) for law (iv). (These are the limits of the descent equations as \(t \to \infty\); an arrow that hits the ground first never reaches them.)

Runge–Kutta solution

Mathews solves each case with the RK4 scheme of the Description, starting from \(y(0) = 0\), \(y'(0) = 300\). In Examples 2–4 he uses \([a, b] = [0, 20]\) with \(n = 100\) steps, so \(h = 0.2\) s, and plots the height \(y(t)\) in the window \(0 \le t \le 20\), \(0 \le y \le 1500\); the images of Example 1’s inputs and plots are lost. In Examples 1–3 he finds the top of the flight and the moment of impact by solving \(v(t) = 0\) and \(y(t) = 0\) with a root finder (FindRoot in Mathematica) applied to the closed-form solution. The values below were located in the same way: from the closed forms with a root finder, and checked against an independent high-accuracy integration (scipy’s DOP853 with relative tolerance \(10^{-13}\) and event detection) and, for Example 4, against quadrature formulas.

Example 1 — No air resistance

An arrow is shot upward from the origin at \(300\) ft/s with no air resistance: \(m\,\dfrac{dv}{dt} = -mg\), that is

\[ y'' = -32, \qquad y(0) = 0, \quad y'(0) = 300 . \]

Find the velocity and position, the ascent time, the descent time, the maximum height and the impact velocity.

Step 1: Integrate twice:

\[ v(t) = 300 - 32t, \qquad y(t) = 300t - 16t^2 . \]

Step 2: The top of the flight is where \(v(t) = 0\):

\[ t_{\max} = \frac{300}{32} = 9.375 \text{ s}, \qquad y_{\max} = y(9.375) = 1406.25 \text{ ft}. \]

Step 3: Impact is where \(y(t) = 0\) with \(t > 0\):

\[ t_{\text{impact}} = \frac{300}{16} = 18.75 \text{ s}, \qquad v(18.75) = -300 \text{ ft/s}. \]

The descent time is \(18.75 - 9.375 = 9.375\) s, equal to the ascent time, and the arrow lands at the speed it was fired, as the formulas of the previous section require. Mathews notes that the maximum comes near \(t = 9\) and the impact near \(t = 19\).

Verification: \(v' = -32\), \(y(0) = 0\), \(v(0) = 300\). The exact solution is a polynomial of degree two, and RK4 with \(h = 0.2\) (the step of Examples 2–4) reproduces it at every grid point on \([0, 20]\) to within \(1.3 \times 10^{-12}\) ft (rounding error). (The legacy pages for this example have lost their output images, so Mathews’ printed values cannot be compared.)

Example 2 — Drag proportional to the velocity

The same arrow, with drag \(F_R = 0.04\,m v\), so \(k/m = 0.04 = \tfrac1{25}\):

\[ y'' = -32 - \frac{y'}{25}, \qquad y(0) = 0, \quad y'(0) = 300 . \]

Step 1: The velocity equation \(v' = -32 - v/25\) is linear. With \(v(0) = 300\),

\[ v(t) = -800 + 1100\, e^{-t/25}. \]

Step 2: Integrate from \(0\) with \(y(0) = 0\):

\[ y(t) = 27500 - 27500\, e^{-t/25} - 800\, t . \]

Step 3: Top of the flight: \(v(t) = 0\) gives \(e^{-t/25} = \tfrac{8}{11}\), so

\[ t_{\max} = 25 \ln\tfrac{11}{8} = 7.96134 \text{ s}, \qquad y_{\max} = 1130.93 \text{ ft}. \]

Step 4: Impact: the root of \(y(t) = 0\) near \(t = 17\) is

\[ t_{\text{impact}} = 16.8675 \text{ s}, \qquad v(t_{\text{impact}}) = -239.760 \text{ ft/s}. \]

The descent time is \(16.8675 - 7.96134 = 8.90615\) s, longer than the ascent time, and the impact speed is below the launch speed. As \(t \to \infty\), \(v(t) \to -800\) ft/s, the terminal velocity \(-mg/k\); at impact the arrow is still far from it. Mathews’ values (\(7.96134\), \(1130.93\), \(16.8675\), \(-239.76\), \(8.90615\)) agree.

Verification: \(v'(t) = -44\, e^{-t/25} = -\tfrac1{25}\bigl(v(t) + 800\bigr) = -32 - \tfrac{v(t)}{25}\) and \(v(0) = -800 + 1100 = 300\). Mathews’ RK4 solution (\(h = 0.2\)) differs from \(y(t)\) by at most \(3.4 \times 10^{-7}\) ft at the grid points of \([0, 20]\), so the two curves coincide in his comparison plot.

Example 3 — Drag proportional to the square of the velocity

The same arrow, with drag \(F_R = \tfrac{1}{2048}\, m v^2\), so \(k/m = \tfrac1{2048}\):

\[ m \frac{dv}{dt} = \mp k v^2 - mg, \qquad y'' = -\frac{y'\lvert y'\rvert}{2048} - 32, \qquad y(0) = 0,\ y'(0) = 300, \]

with the upper sign on the ascent and the lower sign on the descent.

Step 1 (ascent): On the way up, \(v' = -32 - v^2/2048\), whose solution with \(v(0) = 300\) is

\[ V(t) = 256 \tan\!\left(\frac{t_{\max} - t}{8}\right), \qquad t_{\max} = 8 \arctan\tfrac{75}{64}, \]

and integrating with \(Y(0) = 0\),

\[ Y(t) = 2048 \ln\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{9721}}{64}\, \cos\!\left(\frac{t_{\max} - t}{8}\right)\right). \]

Step 2 (top): \(V(t_{\max}) = 0\), with

\[ t_{\max} = 8 \arctan\tfrac{75}{64} = 6.91496 \text{ s}, \qquad y_{\max} = Y(t_{\max}) = 1024 \ln\frac{9721}{4096} = 885.020 \text{ ft}. \]

Step 3 (the wrong continuation): Mathews first uses the ascent formula \(Y(t)\) for the whole flight, as if the drag kept pointing down. Then \(Y\) is symmetric about \(t_{\max}\): it returns to \(0\) at \(t = 2t_{\max} = 13.8299\) s with \(V = -300\) ft/s, so the ascent and descent times are both \(6.91496\) s, as with no air at all. Worse, \(V(t)\) has a vertical asymptote at \(t = t_{\max} + 4\pi = 19.4813\) s, where \(V \to -\infty\): a “drag” that pulls downward makes the fall accelerate without limit. With air resistance the descent must take longer than the ascent, and on the descent the \(v^2\) term must have a positive sign.

Step 4 (descent): On the way down, \(u' = -32 + u^2/2048\) with \(u(t_{\max}) = 0\) and \(Z(t_{\max}) = y_{\max}\). Its solution is

\[ U(t) = -256 \tanh\!\left(\frac{t - t_{\max}}{8}\right), \qquad Z(t) = y_{\max} - 2048 \ln \cosh\!\left(\frac{t - t_{\max}}{8}\right). \]

Mathews prints these as \(U(t) = \dfrac{256\,(1 - e^{(t - t_{\max})/4})}{1 + e^{(t - t_{\max})/4}}\) and \(Z(t) = 4074.82 + 256\,t - 2048 \ln\bigl(5.63355 + e^{t/4}\bigr)\), where \(5.63355 = e^{t_{\max}/4}\) and \(4074.82\) is \(y_{\max} + 256\, t_{\max} + 2048 \ln 2 = 4074.816\) rounded.

Step 5 (impact): \(Z(t) = 0\) when \(\cosh\bigl((t - t_{\max})/8\bigr) = e^{y_{\max}/2048}\), so the descent time is

\[ t_{\text{impact}} - t_{\max} = 8\, \operatorname{arccosh}\!\left(e^{y_{\max}/2048}\right) = 7.98274 \text{ s}, \qquad t_{\text{impact}} = 14.8977 \text{ s}, \qquad U(t_{\text{impact}}) = -194.736 \text{ ft/s}. \]

Now the descent is longer than the ascent, and as \(t \to \infty\), \(U(t) \to -256\) ft/s \(= -\sqrt{mg/k}\), a finite terminal velocity. Mathews’ values (\(6.91496\), \(885.02\), \(14.8977\), \(-194.736\), \(7.98274\)) agree. He notes the maximum near \(t = 7\) and the impact near \(t = 15\).

Step 6 (Runge–Kutta): None of this case analysis is needed numerically. The single equation \(y'' = -32 - y'\lvert y'\rvert/2048\) switches the sign of the drag by itself when \(y'\) changes sign, and Mathews’ RK4 solution with \(h = 0.2\) follows the two-piece closed form \(Y\), \(Z\) to within \(1.7 \times 10^{-4}\) ft at every grid point while the arrow is in the air (\(0 \le t \le 14.8\)). He plots the analytic curves alone in \([0, 17] \times [0, 1200]\), and the RK4 and analytic solutions together in \([0, 15] \times [0, 900]\).

Verification: \(V' = -32 \sec^2\!\bigl(\tfrac{t_{\max} - t}{8}\bigr) = -32\bigl(1 + \tfrac{V^2}{65536}\bigr) = -32 - \tfrac{V^2}{2048}\), and \(V(0) = 256 \cdot \tfrac{75}{64} = 300\). Likewise \(U' = -32\,\operatorname{sech}^2\!\bigl(\tfrac{t - t_{\max}}{8}\bigr) = -32 + \tfrac{U^2}{2048}\), with \(U(t_{\max}) = 0\).

Example 4 — Drag proportional to the 3/2 power of the velocity

The same arrow, with drag of magnitude \(0.005\, m \lvert v \rvert^{3/2}\), so \(k/m = \tfrac1{200}\):

\[ y'' = -\frac{1}{200}\, y' \sqrt{\lvert y'\rvert} - 32, \qquad y(0) = 0, \quad y'(0) = 300 . \]

Mathews’ attempt at a closed-form solution with Mathematica’s DSolve does not produce a usable formula (dropping the initial condition gets it only a little further), so he relies on the RK4 solution (\([0, 20]\), \(n = 100\), \(h = 0.2\)), plotted in \([0, 20] \times [0, 1500]\). He asks for the velocity, the position and a plot; we also located the top and the impact.

Step 1: Top of the flight and impact (event detection on a DOP853 solution, confirmed by quadrature: \(t_{\max} = \int_0^{300} \frac{dv}{32 + 0.005\,v^{3/2}}\) and \(y_{\max} = \int_0^{300} \frac{v\,dv}{32 + 0.005\,v^{3/2}}\), then the impact speed \(U\) from \(\int_0^{U} \frac{u\,du}{32 - 0.005\,u^{3/2}} = y_{\max}\)):

\[ t_{\max} = 7.31383 \text{ s}, \quad y_{\max} = 984.199 \text{ ft}, \quad t_{\text{impact}} = 15.7413 \text{ s}, \quad v(t_{\text{impact}}) = -210.634 \text{ ft/s}. \]

The descent time is \(8.42746\) s, longer than the ascent. The terminal speed of the descent equation is \((32 \cdot 200)^{2/3} = 344.7\) ft/s.

Step 2: Mathews expects the graph to lie between those of Examples 2 and 3, and it does; his comparison plot shows the three curves in \([0, 20] \times [0, 1500]\). On a grid of \(200\,001\) points in \(0.001 \le t \le 20\), \(y_3(t) < y_4(t) < y_2(t)\) at every point. This is not obvious from the drag laws, because the forces are not ordered at every speed: \(0.005\,v^{3/2}\) exceeds \(v^2/2048\) below \(v = 104.86\) ft/s and is less than \(0.04\,v\) below \(v = 64\) ft/s. Along these trajectories the ordering of the heights still holds throughout.

Verification: Mathews’ RK4 heights differ from the high-accuracy solution by at most \(1.3 \times 10^{-3}\) ft at the grid points while the arrow is in the air, and the two independent locations of \(t_{\max}\), \(y_{\max}\), \(t_{\text{impact}}\) and \(v(t_{\text{impact}})\) agree to the digits shown.

The trajectory of a baseball

Now the projectile moves in the plane, with horizontal position \(x(t)\) and height \(y(t)\). Air resistance acts along the velocity \(\mathbf{v} = (x', y')\), opposite to it. If its magnitude is \(k\lvert\mathbf{v}\rvert^2\) per unit mass, its direction is \(-\mathbf{v}/\lvert\mathbf{v}\rvert\) and the force per unit mass is \(-k\lvert\mathbf{v}\rvert\,\mathbf{v}\), whose components are

\[ -k\, x' \sqrt{(x')^2 + (y')^2} \quad\text{and}\quad -k\, y' \sqrt{(x')^2 + (y')^2}. \]

NoteDefinition — Planar drag models (Projectile Motion II)
  1. No air resistance: \(\quad x'' = 0, \qquad y'' = -g\).

  1. Drag proportional to the velocity: \(\quad x'' = -k x', \qquad y'' = -k y' - g\).

  2. Drag proportional to the square of the velocity:

\[ x'' = -k\, x' \sqrt{(x')^2 + (y')^2}, \qquad y'' = -k\, y' \sqrt{(x')^2 + (y')^2} - g . \]

Models (v) and (vi) separate into one equation for \(x\) and one for \(y\); model (vii) couples them through the speed.

Runge–Kutta method for two second-order equations

For \(x'' = f(t, x, x', y, y')\), \(y'' = f_2(t, x, x', y, y')\) with \(x(a) = \alpha\), \(x'(a) = \beta\), \(y(a) = \gamma\), \(y'(a) = \delta\), the state is \(\mathbf{Z} = (x, x', y, y')\) and \(\mathbf{F}(t, \mathbf{Z}) = \bigl(x',\ f,\ y',\ f_2\bigr)\). The RK4 step is the same formula as in the Description, applied to these four components. Mathews launches the ball from \((0, Y_0)\) with speed \(V\) (ft/s) at angle \(\theta\) (degrees), so \(x'(0) = V\cos\theta\) and \(y'(0) = V\sin\theta\). Each case runs from \(a = 0\) to a final time \(b\) with \(n = 100\) steps and prints the final point \((x(b), y(b))\) and the launch speed in miles per hour, \(V \cdot 60/88\). His trajectory plots of \(y\) against \(x\) use Mathematica’s automatic plot ranges.

Mathews aims the cases at four long hits that he quotes: Willie Stargell’s blasts out of Dodger Stadium of \(506.5\) ft (1969) and \(470\) ft (1973), which give landing points \((506.5, 0)\) and \((470, 0)\), and two balls that struck high above the field, Mickey Mantle’s of May 22, 1963 at \(370\) ft from home plate and \(115\) ft up, and Dick Allen’s of July 6, 1974 at \(415\) ft and \(85\) ft, which give the points \((370, 115)\) and \((415, 85)\).

Example 7 — Drag proportional to the square of the velocity

\[ x''(t) = -k\, x'(t)\sqrt{(x'(t))^2 + (y'(t))^2}, \qquad y''(t) = -k\, y'(t)\sqrt{(x'(t))^2 + (y'(t))^2} - 32, \qquad k = 0.002 . \]

Mathews again chooses \(k = 0.002\) arbitrarily and fits four cases to the same four data points. He gives no closed-form solution for this coupled system; the RK4 solution is used. As \(t \to \infty\) the velocity of a ball in this model tends to \((0, -\sqrt{g/k})\), a terminal speed of \(\sqrt{g/k} = 126.5\) ft/s.

Example 8 — A model that is not quadratic drag

Mathews contrasts the quadratic drag model of Example 7 with the plausible-looking but wrong system

\[ x''(t) = -k\,\bigl(x'(t)\bigr)^2, \qquad y''(t) = -k\,\bigl(y'(t)\bigr)^2 - 32, \qquad k = 0.002, \]

with the same initial conditions as the first case of Example 7. (Mathews’ text introduces it as “the model in Example 3”, but the comparison and his concluding sentence refer to Example 7.) As Example 7’s inputs are lost, we use our reconstructed case 1: \(Y_0 = 3\), \(V = 206.79\) ft/s, \(\theta = 42^\circ\).

Step 1: Compare the two at the time \(t = 6.3536\) s at which the quadratic drag ball lands at \((506.5, 0)\). The wrong model’s ball is then at \((541.37,\ 30.55)\), still in the air. It peaks at \((348.73,\ 199.73)\) instead of \((296.71,\ 165.96)\) and lands at \(x = 553.27\) ft at \(t = 6.5850\) s.

Step 2: Why it differs. The quadratic drag force per unit mass is \(-k\lvert\mathbf{v}\rvert\,\mathbf{v}\), whose components were derived above: the speed \(\lvert\mathbf{v}\rvert = \sqrt{(x')^2 + (y')^2}\) multiplies each velocity component. The wrong model replaces \(\lvert\mathbf{v}\rvert\, x'\) by \((x')^2\), which equals \(\lvert x'\rvert\, x'\) while \(x' > 0\), and \(\lvert\mathbf{v}\rvert\, y'\) by \((y')^2 = \lvert y'\rvert^2\), which has the wrong sign whenever \(y' < 0\). So:

  • Its drag is too small: \(\lvert x'\rvert\) and \(\lvert y'\rvert\) are each at most the speed, so on the way up the ball is slowed less, climbs higher and travels farther.
  • On the way down its vertical “drag” \(-k(y')^2\) points down instead of up. The vertical equation is then \(y'' = -g - k(y')^2\), the ascent equation of Example 3 used on the descent, whose velocity \(-\sqrt{g/k}\,\tan\bigl(\sqrt{gk}\,(t - t_{\text{top}})\bigr)\) would become infinite at \(t = 9.491\) s. The ball lands with vertical velocity \(-139.89\) ft/s, already faster than the terminal speed \(126.5\) ft/s of the correct model, at a total speed of \(148.84\) ft/s against \(95.51\) ft/s for the correct model.

Verification: The wrong model’s position at \(t = 6.3536\) s from Mathews’ RK4 with \(n = 100\) agrees with the DOP853 solution to four decimals, and the vertical velocity at landing from the closed form above, \(-139.89\) ft/s, agrees with the numerical value.
