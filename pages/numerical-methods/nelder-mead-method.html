Nelder Mead Method

← Numerical Methods

Source inspiration: (Mathew 2000-2019).

Description

The Nelder-Mead method searches for a local minimum of a function \(f\) of \(n\) variables using only values of \(f\), never its derivatives. It keeps a simplex of \(n + 1\) points, which for two variables is a triangle. At each step the vertices are ranked by their function values as best \(\mathbf{B}\), good \(\mathbf{G}\) and worst \(\mathbf{W}\), and \(\mathbf{W}\) is replaced by a better point on the line from \(\mathbf{W}\) through the midpoint \(\mathbf{M}\) of the opposite side. If the test points on that line fail, the whole triangle is shrunk toward \(\mathbf{B}\). In the examples below the triangles change shape as they move downhill and become much smaller as they close in on a minimum: in the 50-step run of Example 1 the last triangle is more than \(100{,}000\) times smaller across than the first. Nelder and Mead introduced the method, and it is also known as the polytope method.

The candidate points are the reflection \(\mathbf{R}\) of \(\mathbf{W}\) through \(\mathbf{M}\), the expansion \(\mathbf{E}\) beyond it, and two contraction points \(\mathbf{C}_1\) and \(\mathbf{C}_2\) halfway between \(\mathbf{M}\) and \(\mathbf{W}\) or \(\mathbf{R}\):

\[ \mathbf{M} = \frac{\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{G}}{2}, \qquad \mathbf{R} = 2\mathbf{M} - \mathbf{W}, \qquad \mathbf{E} = 2\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{M}, \qquad \mathbf{C}_1 = \frac{\mathbf{W} + \mathbf{M}}{2}, \qquad \mathbf{C}_2 = \frac{\mathbf{M} + \mathbf{R}}{2}. \]

The shrink replaces \(\mathbf{G}\) by \(\mathbf{M}\) and \(\mathbf{W}\) by \(\mathbf{S} = (\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{W})/2\). Mathews works in two variables. He gives two sets of rules for choosing among these points, the decision table of his textbook and the shorter rule of his Mathematica program, and both are set out below. Every run on this page has been computed in exact rational arithmetic.

The Triangle and Its Test Points

NoteDefinition — The triangle \(\mathbf{BGW}\)

Let \(f(x, y)\) be the function to be minimised, and let \(\mathbf{V}_k = (x_k, y_k)\), \(k = 1, 2, 3\), be the vertices of a triangle. Evaluate \(z_k = f(x_k, y_k)\) and renumber the vertices so that \(z_1 \le z_2 \le z_3\). Then \[ \mathbf{B} = (x_1, y_1), \qquad \mathbf{G} = (x_2, y_2), \qquad \mathbf{W} = (x_3, y_3) \tag{1} \] are the best, good (next to best) and worst vertices.

Midpoint of the good side. The construction uses the midpoint of the side \(\mathbf{BG}\), the side opposite \(\mathbf{W}\): \[ \mathbf{M} = \frac{\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{G}}{2} = \left( \frac{x_1 + x_2}{2},\ \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2} \right). \tag{2} \]

Reflection. The values fall from \(\mathbf{W}\) to \(\mathbf{B}\) and from \(\mathbf{W}\) to \(\mathbf{G}\), so \(f\) may well take smaller values on the far side of the line \(\mathbf{BG}\), away from \(\mathbf{W}\). The test point \(\mathbf{R}\) is found by reflecting the triangle through the side \(\mathbf{BG}\): if \(d\) is the length of the segment from \(\mathbf{W}\) to \(\mathbf{M}\), extend that segment a further distance \(d\) through \(\mathbf{M}\). In vector form, \[ \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{M} + (\mathbf{M} - \mathbf{W}) = 2\mathbf{M} - \mathbf{W}. \tag{3} \]

Expansion. If \(f(\mathbf{R})\) is smaller than \(f(\mathbf{W})\), the move was in a good direction, and the minimum may lie a little farther on. Extend the segment through \(\mathbf{M}\) and \(\mathbf{R}\) by another distance \(d\) to the point \[ \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{R} + (\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{M}) = 2\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{M}, \tag{4} \] which forms the expanded triangle \(\mathbf{BGE}\).

Contraction. If \(f(\mathbf{R})\) is no better than \(f(\mathbf{W})\), another point must be tested. The function may be smaller at \(\mathbf{M}\), but \(\mathbf{W}\) cannot be replaced by \(\mathbf{M}\), because \(\mathbf{B}\), \(\mathbf{G}\) and \(\mathbf{M}\) lie on one line and would not form a triangle. Instead, take the midpoints of the segments \(\mathbf{WM}\) and \(\mathbf{MR}\): \[ \mathbf{C}_1 = \frac{\mathbf{W} + \mathbf{M}}{2}, \qquad \mathbf{C}_2 = \frac{\mathbf{M} + \mathbf{R}}{2}. \tag{5} \] In the textbook rules, the one with the smaller function value is called \(\mathbf{C}\), and the new triangle is \(\mathbf{BGC}\). Mathews notes that the choice between \(\mathbf{C}_1\) and \(\mathbf{C}_2\) may seem unnecessary in two dimensions but matters in higher dimensions.

Shrink toward \(\mathbf{B}\). If \(f(\mathbf{C})\) is not less than \(f(\mathbf{W})\), the points \(\mathbf{G}\) and \(\mathbf{W}\) are shrunk toward \(\mathbf{B}\). \(\mathbf{G}\) is replaced by \(\mathbf{M}\), the midpoint of \(\mathbf{BG}\), and \(\mathbf{W}\) by the midpoint of \(\mathbf{BW}\), \[ \mathbf{S} = \frac{\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{W}}{2}. \tag{6} \]

All the test points lie on the line through \(\mathbf{W}\) and \(\mathbf{M}\), at \(\mathbf{M} + t\,(\mathbf{M} - \mathbf{W})\) with \(t = 1\) for \(\mathbf{R}\), \(t = 2\) for \(\mathbf{E}\), \(t = -\tfrac12\) for \(\mathbf{C}_1\) and \(t = \tfrac12\) for \(\mathbf{C}_2\), and the shrink halves the distances from \(\mathbf{B}\). In general terms, these are reflection, expansion, contraction and shrink coefficients of \(1\), \(2\), \(\tfrac12\) and \(\tfrac12\).

More variables. Mathews states the algorithm for a continuous function \(f(x_1, \ldots, x_n)\) of \(n\) variables, starting from \(n + 1\) points \(\mathbf{V}_k = (v_{k,1}, \ldots, v_{k,n})\), \(k = 1, \ldots, n + 1\). In the general form of the method the vertices are ordered so that \(f(\mathbf{V}_1) \le \cdots \le f(\mathbf{V}_{n+1})\). The worst vertex \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{V}_{n+1}\) is then moved along the line through the centroid of the other \(n\) vertices, \[ \mathbf{M} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \mathbf{V}_k, \] with formulas (3) to (5) unchanged, and the shrink replaces every \(\mathbf{V}_k\), \(k \ge 2\), by \((\mathbf{V}_1 + \mathbf{V}_k)/2\). For \(n = 2\) this is the triangle construction above.

Logical Decisions for Each Step

An efficient implementation evaluates \(f\) only where it needs to. Each step finds one new vertex to replace \(\mathbf{W}\) (or performs the shrink), and the step ends as soon as that vertex is found. Mathews’ textbook (Mathews and Fink, Numerical Methods Using MATLAB, 4th edition, 2004, Section 8.2, Table 8.5, which the module’s proof page links) gives the decisions as two cases:

ImportantAlgorithm — Nelder-Mead step, textbook rules

Order the vertices as \(\mathbf{B}, \mathbf{G}, \mathbf{W}\), and compute \(\mathbf{M}\), \(\mathbf{R}\) and \(f(\mathbf{R})\).

Case (i), \(f(\mathbf{R}) < f(\mathbf{G})\) (reflect or expand):

  • if \(f(\mathbf{B}) < f(\mathbf{R})\), replace \(\mathbf{W}\) with \(\mathbf{R}\);
  • otherwise compute \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(f(\mathbf{E})\). If \(f(\mathbf{E}) < f(\mathbf{B})\), replace \(\mathbf{W}\) with \(\mathbf{E}\); otherwise replace \(\mathbf{W}\) with \(\mathbf{R}\).

Case (ii), \(f(\mathbf{R}) \ge f(\mathbf{G})\) (contract or shrink):

  • if \(f(\mathbf{R}) < f(\mathbf{W})\), replace \(\mathbf{W}\) with \(\mathbf{R}\);
  • compute \(\mathbf{C}_1 = (\mathbf{W} + \mathbf{M})/2\) and \(\mathbf{C}_2 = (\mathbf{M} + \mathbf{R})/2\), and let \(\mathbf{C}\) be the one with the smaller value of \(f\);
  • if \(f(\mathbf{C}) < f(\mathbf{W})\), replace \(\mathbf{W}\) with \(\mathbf{C}\); otherwise compute \(\mathbf{S} = (\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{W})/2\), replace \(\mathbf{W}\) with \(\mathbf{S}\) and \(\mathbf{G}\) with \(\mathbf{M}\).

Read as printed, case (ii) goes on to the contraction even after \(\mathbf{W}\) has been replaced by \(\mathbf{R}\). In that event the new \(\mathbf{W}\) is \(\mathbf{R}\), and both contraction points are the midpoint of \(\mathbf{MR}\).

The Mathematica subroutine Nelder2D in Mathews’ module, which produces the runs in every example whose output survives, uses a shorter rule. It tests the candidates in a fixed order and takes the first one that is better than \(\mathbf{W}\):

ImportantAlgorithm — Nelder-Mead step, Mathews’ subroutine

Compute \(\mathbf{M}\), \(\mathbf{R}\), \(\mathbf{E}\), and then, as needed, \(\mathbf{C}_1\) and \(\mathbf{C}_2\).

  1. If \(f(\mathbf{E}) < f(\mathbf{W})\), replace \(\mathbf{W}\) with \(\mathbf{E}\).
  2. Otherwise, if \(f(\mathbf{R}) < f(\mathbf{W})\), replace \(\mathbf{W}\) with \(\mathbf{R}\).
  3. Otherwise, if \(f(\mathbf{C}_1) < f(\mathbf{W})\), replace \(\mathbf{W}\) with \(\mathbf{C}_1\).
  4. Otherwise, if \(f(\mathbf{C}_2) < f(\mathbf{W})\), replace \(\mathbf{W}\) with \(\mathbf{C}_2\).
  5. Otherwise shrink: replace \(\mathbf{W}\) with \((\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{W})/2\) and \(\mathbf{G}\) with \((\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{G})/2 = \mathbf{M}\).

Then reorder the three vertices as \(\mathbf{B}, \mathbf{G}, \mathbf{W}\).

The two rules use the same points but can choose differently:

  • The subroutine accepts \(\mathbf{E}\) whenever it is better than \(\mathbf{W}\), even when \(\mathbf{R}\) is better still. The textbook expands only when \(f(\mathbf{R}) \le f(\mathbf{B})\), and keeps \(\mathbf{E}\) only if \(f(\mathbf{E}) < f(\mathbf{B})\). Step 3 of Example 4 shows the subroutine taking a worse point this way.
  • The subroutine accepts \(\mathbf{R}\) whenever it is better than \(\mathbf{W}\). The textbook, when \(f(\mathbf{G}) \le f(\mathbf{R}) < f(\mathbf{W})\), replaces \(\mathbf{W}\) with \(\mathbf{R}\) and then still tries a contraction.
  • The subroutine tries \(\mathbf{C}_1\) before \(\mathbf{C}_2\). The textbook takes the better of the two.

Stopping. Mathews’ first subroutine runs a fixed number of steps. His “more efficient” version stores the function values instead of recomputing them and stops as soon as \[ f(\mathbf{W}) \le f(\mathbf{B}) + \epsilon . \tag{7} \] It always takes at least one step and never more than \(99\). This is a test on function values only: it stops when the three vertices have nearly equal values, not when the triangle is small.

What every step guarantees. These facts hold for both rules and any \(f\). A step that is not a shrink replaces \(\mathbf{W}\) by a point whose value is less than \(f(\mathbf{W})\), because each replacement above is made only after such a test. No step removes \(\mathbf{B}\) unless it finds a point with a smaller value, and a shrink keeps \(\mathbf{B}\). So \(f(\mathbf{B})\) never increases. Nothing in the rules makes the triangle converge to a minimum, though: the method is a search heuristic, and the module gives no convergence theorem.

WarningTies and round-off

All the tests are strict inequalities, so when two values are equal the result depends on how the tie is broken, and in floating-point arithmetic on how the rounding falls. In the runs below, vertices with equal values are listed with the smaller \(x\)-coordinate first, then the smaller \(y\), and \(\mathbf{C}_1\) is taken when \(f(\mathbf{C}_1) = f(\mathbf{C}_2)\). Example 1 meets exact ties within its first six steps. Its \(f\) is symmetric about the minimum, \(f(3 + u, 2 + v) = f(3 - u, 2 - v)\), and each tie is between two points that are mirror images through \((3, 2)\). Mathews’ machine-precision run takes a different path from the sixth step on (see Example 4).

Example 1 — A quadratic function

Use the Nelder-Mead method to find the minimum of \[ f(x, y) = x^2 - 4x + y^2 - y - xy \] starting with the vertices \(\mathbf{V}_1 = (0, 0)\), \(\mathbf{V}_2 = (1.2, 0.0)\) and \(\mathbf{V}_3 = (0.0, 0.8)\).

The exact answer. Setting the gradient \((2x - 4 - y,\ 2y - 1 - x)\) to zero gives \((x, y) = (3, 2)\), where \(f(3, 2) = -7\). Writing \(x = 3 + u\), \(y = 2 + v\), \[ f(3 + u, 2 + v) = -7 + u^2 - uv + v^2 = -7 + \left(u - \tfrac{v}{2}\right)^2 + \tfrac34 v^2, \] so \((3, 2)\) is the only minimum. The Hessian \(\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) has eigenvalues \(1\) and \(3\).

Step 1 (evaluate and order). The function takes the values \[ f(0, 0) = 0.0, \qquad f(1.2, 0.0) = -3.36, \qquad f(0.0, 0.8) = -0.16, \] so \[ \mathbf{B} = (1.2, 0.0), \qquad \mathbf{G} = (0.0, 0.8), \qquad \mathbf{W} = (0, 0). \]

Step 2 (midpoint and reflection). The vertex \(\mathbf{W} = (0, 0)\) will be replaced. \[ \mathbf{M} = \frac{\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{G}}{2} = (0.6, 0.4), \qquad \mathbf{R} = 2\mathbf{M} - \mathbf{W} = (1.2, 0.8), \qquad f(\mathbf{R}) = -4.48 . \]

Step 3 (decide). \(f(\mathbf{R}) = -4.48\) is less than \(f(\mathbf{G}) = -0.16\), so this is case (i). Since \(f(\mathbf{R}) \le f(\mathbf{B}) = -3.36\), the move is in the right direction, and the expansion point is constructed: \[ \mathbf{E} = 2\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{M} = 2(1.2, 0.8) - (0.6, 0.4) = (1.8, 1.2), \qquad f(\mathbf{E}) = -5.88 . \]

Step 4 (replace). \(f(\mathbf{E}) = -5.88\) is less than \(f(\mathbf{B})\), so \(\mathbf{W}\) is replaced with \(\mathbf{E}\), and the new triangle has vertices \[ \boxed{\ \mathbf{V}_1 = (1.8, 1.2), \qquad \mathbf{V}_2 = (1.2, 0.0), \qquad \mathbf{V}_3 = (0.0, 0.8)\ } \] with values \(-5.88\), \(-3.36\) and \(-0.16\). Mathews’ subroutine makes the same first step, since its first test, \(f(\mathbf{E}) = -5.88 < f(\mathbf{W}) = 0\), already succeeds.

Example 2 — Rosenbrock’s parabolic valley

Use the Nelder-Mead method to find the minimum of \[ f(x, y) = 100\,(y - x^2)^2 + (1 - x)^2 . \] Mathews calls this Rosenbrock’s parabolic valley, from about 1960. As a sum of squares, \(f \ge 0\), and \(f = 0\) only where \(y = x^2\) and \(x = 1\), so the minimum is \(f(1, 1) = 0\). The minimum lies at the end of a long, curved valley along \(y = x^2\). On the valley floor \(f(1 - t, (1 - t)^2) = t^2\), while across the floor \(f\) rises steeply: the Hessian at \((1, 1)\), \(\begin{pmatrix} 802 & -400 \\ -400 & 200 \end{pmatrix}\), has eigenvalues \(0.3994\) and \(1001.6\).

All that survives is his remark that even Mathematica’s FindMinimum “is having a hard time”, because \(f\) is flat near the minimum, and his advice to raise the working precision.

Steps worth following.

  • Step 3 expands to a worse point. With \(\mathbf{W} = (1.2, 0)\) and \(\mathbf{M} = (2.4, 0.8)\), the reflection \(\mathbf{R} = (3.6, 1.6)\) has \(f(\mathbf{R}) = -6.24\), lower even than \(f(\mathbf{B}) = -5.88\). The subroutine tests \(\mathbf{E} = (4.8, 2.4)\) first, finds \(f(\mathbf{E}) = -4.32 < f(\mathbf{W}) = -3.36\), and takes \(\mathbf{E}\), which becomes the new worst vertex. The textbook rules would have taken \(\mathbf{R}\) here, since \(f(\mathbf{E}) > f(\mathbf{B})\).
  • Step 4 recovers that point. \(\mathbf{B}\) and \(\mathbf{G}\) are unchanged, so \(\mathbf{M} = (2.4, 0.8)\) again. Now \(\mathbf{R} = (0, -0.8)\) with \(f = 1.44\) and \(\mathbf{E} = (-2.4, -2.4)\) with \(f = 17.76\) both fail, and \(\mathbf{C}_1 = (\mathbf{W} + \mathbf{M})/2 = (3.6, 1.6)\), the point step 3 passed over, is accepted. From here on the subroutine’s triangle after \(k + 1\) steps is the textbook’s triangle after \(k\) steps in Example 1, for every \(k\) from \(3\) to \(9\): step 3 cost one step.
  • Step 6 meets a tie. With \(\mathbf{W} = (1.8, 1.2)\) and \(\mathbf{M} = (3, 2)\), the reflection \(\mathbf{R} = (4.2, 2.8)\) is the mirror image of \(\mathbf{W}\) through the minimum, and \(f(\mathbf{R}) = f(\mathbf{W}) = -5.88\) exactly. \(\mathbf{E} = (5.4, 3.6)\) has \(f = -2.52\), so neither \(\mathbf{E}\) nor \(\mathbf{R}\) passes its strict test, and \(\mathbf{C}_1 = (2.4, 1.6)\), with \(f = -6.72\), is taken.

After \(15\) steps the best vertex is \((2.989709, 1.979285)\), a distance \(0.0231\) from \((3, 2)\), with \(f(\mathbf{B}) = -6.999678\).

Mathews’ frames from step 6 on. Mathews’ printed triangles agree with the table through \(k = 5\), apart from the order of the two tied vertices at \(k = 5\). At step 6 his run accepted \(\mathbf{R} = (4.2, 2.8)\), so in his machine arithmetic \(f(\mathbf{R})\) came out slightly below \(f(\mathbf{W})\), although the two are equal. Because \(f(3 + u, 2 + v) = -7 + u^2 - uv + v^2\) does not change when \((u, v)\) is replaced by \((-u, -v)\), that step turned the triangle into its own mirror image through \((3, 2)\). From then on, his triangle after \(k\) steps is the mirror image through \((3, 2)\) of the exact triangle after \(k - 1\) steps. This was checked for every \(k\) from \(6\) to \(50\), the end of his run in Example 1. For instance his triangle after \(15\) steps, with best vertex \((3.039990, 2.004346)\), is the mirror image of the row \(k = 14\) above.
