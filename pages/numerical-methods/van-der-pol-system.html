van der Pol System

← Numerical Methods

Source inspiration: (Mathew 2000-2019).

Description

The van der Pol equation \(x'' - \mu (1 - x^2)\, x' + x = 0\), with a constant \(\mu \ge 0\), is an oscillator whose damping depends on the displacement \(x\). For \(\mu > 0\), while \(|x| < 1\) the damping coefficient \(-\mu(1 - x^2)\) is negative and pumps energy in, and while \(|x| > 1\) it is positive and drains energy out. For \(\mu = 0\) the equation is the harmonic oscillator \(x'' + x = 0\), whose solutions other than \(x = 0\) run round circles in the phase plane. For every \(\mu > 0\) there is exactly one periodic solution, a closed curve called the limit cycle, and every other solution except the equilibrium at the origin winds onto it, from outside or from inside.

For \(\mu > 0\) Mathews computes the solutions numerically. With \(y = x'\) the equation becomes the first-order system \(x' = y\), \(y' = -x + \mu(1 - x^2)\, y\), and Mathews solves that system with the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method in vector form. With the vector \(\mathbf{Z} = (x, y)\), the right-hand side \(\mathbf{F}(t, \mathbf{Z}) = \bigl(y,\ -x + \mu(1 - x^2)\, y\bigr)\) and a fixed step \(h = (b - a)/n\), each step is

\[ \mathbf{Z}_{j+1} = \mathbf{Z}_j + \frac{1}{6}\bigl(\mathbf{k}_1 + 2\mathbf{k}_2 + 2\mathbf{k}_3 + \mathbf{k}_4\bigr), \]

where the four vectors \(\mathbf{k}_i\) are \(h\) times \(\mathbf{F}\) evaluated at the start, twice at the middle and once at the end of the step (equation (5) below). Mathews’ module has six examples, \(\mu = 0, \tfrac{1}{16}, \tfrac14, \tfrac12, 1, 4\), and plots the solution of Example 1 as a path in the \(xy\)-plane. Example 1 (\(\mu = 0\)) survives in full: it starts at \((x, y) = (0, 3)\) and takes \(n = 400\) steps over one turn, \(0 \le t \le 6.283185\). For Examples 2 to 6 the archive keeps only the value of \(\mu\), so this page keeps Example 1’s starting point and integrates over \(0 \le t \le 60\) with \(n = 6000\) steps (\(h = 0.01\)). Every number below has been recomputed.

The van der Pol Equation

NoteDefinition — van der Pol equation

The van der Pol equation is the second-order differential equation

\[ x'' - \mu \left(1 - x^2\right) x' + x = 0, \tag{1} \]

where \(\mu\) is a constant. Mathews takes \(\mu > 0\) in the definition, and his first example uses \(\mu = 0\).

When \(\mu = 0\) the equation reduces to \(x'' + x = 0\), which has the familiar solution

\[ x(t) = c_1 \cos t + c_2 \sin t . \]

For \(\mu > 0\) the middle term \(-\mu(1 - x^2)\, x'\) acts like friction or resistance where its coefficient \(-\mu(1 - x^2)\) is positive, that is where \(|x| > 1\). Where \(|x| < 1\) the coefficient is negative, the case of “negative resistance”, which feeds energy into the motion. Mathews notes that vacuum-tube circuits of early radios showed such negative resistance; the equation is named after Balthasar van der Pol (1889–1959), who studied it, and its solutions show orbital stability: nearby solutions are drawn onto the same closed path.

The equivalent first-order system

With \(y = x'\), equation (1) becomes the system

\[ x' = y, \qquad y' = -x + \mu\left(1 - x^2\right) y . \tag{2} \]

Energy

The energy of the harmonic oscillator, \(E = \tfrac12 (x^2 + y^2)\), is the squared distance from the origin, halved. Along a solution of (2),

\[ \frac{dE}{dt} = x x' + y y' = x y + y\bigl(-x + \mu(1 - x^2)\, y\bigr) = \mu \left(1 - x^2\right) y^2 . \tag{3} \]

For \(\mu = 0\) the energy is constant, so every solution runs round a circle centred at the origin. For \(\mu > 0\) the energy grows while \(|x| < 1\) and decays while \(|x| > 1\) (wherever \(y \ne 0\)), so small oscillations are pushed outwards and large ones pulled inwards.

The equilibrium at the origin

The only equilibrium of (2) is \((0, 0)\), since \(x' = 0\) forces \(y = 0\) and then \(y' = 0\) forces \(x = 0\). The Jacobian matrix of (2) at the origin and its eigenvalues are

\[ \mathbf{J}(0, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & \mu \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda^2 - \mu \lambda + 1 = 0, \qquad \lambda = \frac{\mu \pm \sqrt{\mu^2 - 4}}{2} . \]

For \(\mu = 0\) the eigenvalues are \(\pm i\) and the origin is a centre, surrounded by the circles. For \(0 < \mu < 2\) they are complex with positive real part \(\mu/2\), so solutions spiral away from the origin; for \(\mu = 2\) there is the double eigenvalue \(1\), and for \(\mu > 2\) both eigenvalues are real and positive, so solutions leave the origin without spiralling. In every case with \(\mu > 0\) the origin repels.

The limit cycle

NoteDefinition — Limit cycle

A limit cycle of a system \(x' = f_1(x, y)\), \(y' = f_2(x, y)\) is a closed path traced by a periodic solution that has no other closed paths arbitrarily close to it. It is stable when the solutions that start near it approach it as \(t \to \infty\).

The circles of the case \(\mu = 0\) are closed paths, but each has others arbitrarily close to it, so none of them is a limit cycle.

TipTheorem — Liénard’s theorem, applied to the van der Pol equation (general)

Write (1) as \(x'' + f(x)\, x' + g(x) = 0\) with \(f(x) = \mu(x^2 - 1)\) and \(g(x) = x\), and let \(F(x) = \int_0^x f(s)\, ds = \mu\bigl(\tfrac13 x^3 - x\bigr)\). Liénard’s theorem gives a unique stable limit cycle when \(F\) and \(g\) have continuous derivatives, \(g\) is odd with \(x g(x) > 0\) for \(x \ne 0\), \(F\) is odd with \(F'(0) < 0\), \(F\) has a single positive zero \(x = a\), and \(F\) increases to \(\infty\) for \(x \ge a\). For \(\mu > 0\) all of these hold, with \(F'(0) = -\mu\), \(a = \sqrt3\) and \(F'(x) = \mu(x^2 - 1) > 0\) for \(x \ge \sqrt3\). So for every \(\mu > 0\) the van der Pol equation has exactly one periodic solution up to a shift in time, and every solution other than the equilibrium \(x = 0\) approaches it as \(t \to \infty\).

The theorem does not say where the cycle is. Its size for small \(\mu\) follows from the energy equation (3): when \(\mu\) is small a solution is nearly a circle \(x = A \sin t\), \(y = A \cos t\) over one turn, and the net energy gained over that turn is

\[ \int_0^{2\pi} \mu \left(1 - A^2 \sin^2 t\right) A^2 \cos^2 t \, dt = \frac{\pi \mu A^2}{4}\left(4 - A^2\right) . \tag{4} \]

This is positive for \(A < 2\) and negative for \(A > 2\), so for small \(\mu\) the limit cycle is close to the circle of radius \(2\). This is an approximation for small \(\mu\); the computed cycles below have their largest \(x\) between \(2.00004\) (for \(\mu = \tfrac{1}{16}\)) and \(2.02296\) (for \(\mu = 4\)), and their period grows from close to \(2\pi\) to \(10.2035\).

The cycles were computed with an adaptive Runge-Kutta method of order 8 at tolerance \(10^{-13}\), run for \(200\) time units from \((2, 0)\) before measuring, and the periods and largest \(x\) were confirmed to all the digits shown by an independent implicit (Radau) solver. The last column is the factor by which the gap between a nearby solution and the cycle shrinks in one turn. For a periodic solution of a plane system, that factor is (a general result) the exponential of the integral of the divergence \(\partial f_1/\partial x + \partial f_2/\partial y = \mu(1 - x^2)\) over one period,

\[ \rho = \exp\!\left( \mu \int_0^P \bigl(1 - x(t)^2\bigr)\, dt \right), \]

and for small \(\mu\), where \(x \approx 2 \cos t\), the integral is close to \(-2\pi\mu\) and \(\rho \approx e^{-2\pi\mu}\) (\(0.6752\) for \(\mu = \tfrac{1}{16}\)). The cycle attracts slowly for small \(\mu\) and extremely fast for \(\mu = 4\), as the examples show.

The Runge-Kutta Method for a System

NoteInitial value problem in the plane

Compute approximations to the solution of

\[ x' = f_1(t, x, y), \quad x(a) = \alpha, \qquad y' = f_2(t, x, y), \quad y(a) = \beta, \qquad a \le t \le b, \]

at a discrete set of points \(t_0 = a < t_1 < \cdots < t_n = b\). In vector form, with \(\mathbf{Z} = (x, y)\) and \(\mathbf{F}(t, \mathbf{Z}) = \bigl(f_1(t, x, y),\ f_2(t, x, y)\bigr)\), the problem is \(\mathbf{Z}' = \mathbf{F}(t, \mathbf{Z})\), \(\mathbf{Z}(a) = (\alpha, \beta)\).

The Runge-Kutta method in two dimensions is the “vector form” of the one-dimensional method: the function \(f\) is replaced by the vector function \(\mathbf{F}\), and every stage is computed for both components at once.

ImportantRunge-Kutta method of order 4 for a system

With \(h = (b - a)/n\), \(t_j = a + j h\) and \(\mathbf{Z}_0 = (\alpha, \beta)\), for \(j = 0, 1, \ldots, n - 1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{k}_1 &= h\, \mathbf{F}(t_j, \mathbf{Z}_j), \\ \mathbf{k}_2 &= h\, \mathbf{F}\bigl(t_j + \tfrac{h}{2},\ \mathbf{Z}_j + \tfrac12 \mathbf{k}_1\bigr), \\ \mathbf{k}_3 &= h\, \mathbf{F}\bigl(t_j + \tfrac{h}{2},\ \mathbf{Z}_j + \tfrac12 \mathbf{k}_2\bigr), \\ \mathbf{k}_4 &= h\, \mathbf{F}\bigl(t_j + h,\ \mathbf{Z}_j + \mathbf{k}_3\bigr), \\ \mathbf{Z}_{j+1} &= \mathbf{Z}_j + \tfrac16 \bigl(\mathbf{k}_1 + 2\mathbf{k}_2 + 2\mathbf{k}_3 + \mathbf{k}_4\bigr). \end{aligned} \tag{5} \]

Mathews’ subroutine builds the list of times \(t_j\) and the list of points \(\mathbf{Z}_j\) and returns the points, which are then joined by line segments to draw the path in the \(xy\)-plane. For a smooth \(\mathbf{F}\) the method has global error of order \(h^4\) (a general result): doubling \(n\) divides the error by about \(16\), as both sets of examples below confirm. For the van der Pol system \(\mathbf{F}\) does not depend on \(t\), so the times \(t_j\) only label the points.

Example 1 — \(\mu = 0\): the harmonic oscillator

Solve the van der Pol equation with \(\mu = 0\),

\[ x'' + x = 0, \qquad x(0) = 0, \quad x'(0) = 3, \]

over \(0 \le t \le 6.283185\) with \(n = 400\) steps. These are the values in Mathews’ notebook.

Step 1: Write the equation as the system (2) with \(\mu = 0\):

\[ x' = y, \qquad y' = -x, \qquad \mathbf{F}(t, (x, y)) = (y, -x), \qquad \mathbf{Z}_0 = (0, 3). \]

Step 2: The step size is

\[ h = \frac{6.283185 - 0}{400} = 0.0157079625 . \]

Step 3: The first step, from \(t_0 = 0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{k}_1 &= h\,\mathbf{F}(0, (0, 3)) = h\,(3, 0) = (0.0471238875,\ 0), \\ \mathbf{k}_2 &= h\,\mathbf{F}\bigl(\tfrac{h}{2}, (0.02356194375,\ 3)\bigr) = (0.0471238875,\ -0.000370110129), \\ \mathbf{k}_3 &= h\,\mathbf{F}\bigl(\tfrac{h}{2}, (0.02356194375,\ 2.999814945)\bigr) = (0.0471209807,\ -0.000370110129), \\ \mathbf{k}_4 &= h\,\mathbf{F}\bigl(h, (0.0471209807,\ 2.999629890)\bigr) = (0.0471180738,\ -0.000740174597), \\ \mathbf{Z}_1 &= \mathbf{Z}_0 + \tfrac16 (\mathbf{k}_1 + 2\mathbf{k}_2 + 2\mathbf{k}_3 + \mathbf{k}_4) = (0.0471219496,\ 2.999629897). \end{aligned} \]

The exact value \(\bigl(3\sin h, 3\cos h\bigr) = (0.0471219496, 2.999629897)\) agrees to all the digits shown; the error in \(x\) is \(-2.4 \times 10^{-11}\).

Step 4: Repeating the step \(400\) times gives the end point

\[ \mathbf{Z}_{400} = \bigl(-9.311010 \times 10^{-7},\ 2.99999999987\bigr) \approx \bigl(-9.31101 \times 10^{-7},\ 3.\bigr), \]

which is the end point Mathews prints, \(\{-9.31101 \times 10^{-7}, 3.\}\).

The exact solution is \(x(t) = 3 \sin t\), \(y(t) = 3 \cos t\), the circle of radius \(3\) traced clockwise from its top point \((0, 3)\). Mathews plots the \(400\) segments in the \(xy\)-plane with both axes marked from \(-3\) to \(3\), and they close up into that circle.

Verification: The right end \(b = 6.283185\) is \(3.07 \times 10^{-7}\) short of \(2\pi\), so the exact solution there is not \((0, 3)\):

Examples 2 to 6 — \(\mu = \tfrac{1}{16}, \tfrac14, \tfrac12, 1, 4\)

Mathews solves the van der Pol equation with \(\mu = \tfrac{1}{16}\) (Example 2), \(\tfrac14\) (Example 3), \(\tfrac12\) (Example 4), \(1\) (Example 5) and \(4\) (Example 6):

\[ x'' - \tfrac{1}{16}\left(1 - x^2\right) x' + x = 0, \quad \ldots, \quad x'' - 4\left(1 - x^2\right) x' + x = 0 . \]

Each is the system (2) with \(\mathbf{F}(t, (x, y)) = \bigl(y,\ -x + \mu(1 - x^2)\, y\bigr)\), solved by (5). For these examples the archive keeps only the value of \(\mu\); the notebook cells with the interval, the number of steps and the starting point, and the plots, are lost. This page therefore keeps Example 1’s starting point \((x, y) = (0, 3)\), that is \(x(0) = 0\), \(x'(0) = 3\), and integrates over \(0 \le t \le 60\) with \(n = 6000\) steps, \(h = 0.01\).

Each solution is followed through the points where \(x\) has a local maximum, which are the points where the path crosses the positive \(x\)-axis (\(y = 0\), \(x > 0\)). Comparing those maxima with the largest \(x\) on the cycle (the table above) measures how far the solution still is from the limit cycle after each turn. The maxima below are located exactly (to the digits shown) with the order-8 reference solver; the Runge-Kutta points agree with it to within the errors listed in the verification table at the end of this section.

Example 2 — \(\mu = \tfrac{1}{16}\)

The cycle passes through the positive \(y\)-axis at \(y = 2.0007\), so \((0, 3)\) is outside it, and the solution spirals inwards. The ten maxima of \(x\) in \(0 \le t \le 60\) are

\[ 2.849009,\ 2.464188,\ 2.279308,\ 2.175986,\ 2.113772,\ 2.074697,\ 2.049523,\ 2.033042,\ 2.022140,\ 2.014879, \]

decreasing towards the cycle’s \(2.000041\). The gap shrinks by the factors \(0.547, 0.602, 0.630, \ldots, 0.671\) per turn, approaching \(\rho = 0.675\), so at the tenth maximum (\(t = 58.12\)) the solution is still \(0.0148\) outside the cycle. This is the slowest approach of the five: it takes many turns of a nearly circular path.

Example 3 — \(\mu = \tfrac14\)

The cycle crosses the positive \(y\)-axis at \(y = 2.0111\), so the solution again starts outside it. The maxima of \(x\) are

\[ 2.593435,\ 2.083519,\ 2.016735,\ 2.003912,\ 2.001313,\ 2.000781,\ 2.000672,\ 2.000650,\ 2.000645,\ 2.000644, \]

decreasing to the cycle’s \(2.000644\); from the sixth maximum on each gap is \(\rho = 0.2053\) times the one before (to four digits), down to \(2.4 \times 10^{-7}\) at the tenth maximum.

Example 4 — \(\mu = \tfrac12\)

The cycle crosses the positive \(y\)-axis at \(y = 2.0441\). The maxima of \(x\) are

\[ 2.418210,\ 2.012688,\ 2.002883,\ 2.002503,\ 2.002489,\ 2.002488, \ldots \]

and from the sixth on they equal the cycle’s \(2.002488\) to six decimals. From the fourth maximum on each gap is \(\rho = 0.0392\) times the one before, until the gap reaches rounding level (\(3.8 \times 10^{-14}\) at the tenth maximum).

Example 5 — \(\mu = 1\)

The cycle crosses the positive \(y\)-axis at \(y = 2.1727\). The maxima of \(x\) are

\[ 2.240070,\ 2.008728,\ 2.008620, \ldots \]

and from the third on (at \(t = 14.70\)) they equal the cycle’s \(2.008620\) to six decimals. The gaps are \(0.23\), \(1.1 \times 10^{-4}\), \(9.3 \times 10^{-8}\), \(8.0 \times 10^{-11}\) and \(6.8 \times 10^{-14}\), and from the third on each is about \(\rho \approx 8.6 \times 10^{-4}\) times the one before; from the sixth maximum on the solution is on the cycle to rounding level. The cycle is visibly no longer a circle: on it \(|y|\) reaches \(2.6784\) while \(|x|\) stays below \(2.0087\).

Example 6 — \(\mu = 4\)

Now the cycle crosses the positive \(y\)-axis at \(y = 3.7623\), so the starting point \((0, 3)\) is inside the cycle. The first maximum of \(x\) is \(1.956754\), below the cycle’s \(2.022963\), and the second, at \(t = 10.47\), already equals \(2.022963\) to rounding level (\(\rho = 1.3 \times 10^{-25}\)). The cycle is a relaxation oscillation, made of slow and fast parts. From its largest value \(2.023\), \(x\) takes \(3.860\) time units, \(76\%\) of the half period \(5.102\), to drift down to \(x = 1\); it then falls from \(1\) to \(-1\) in only \(0.829\) time units, and during that fall, at \(x = -0.9805\), \(|y|\) peaks at \(6.3442\). The other half of the cycle is the mirror image, since (2) is unchanged when \((x, y)\) is replaced by \((-x, -y)\). Over the whole cycle \(|y|\) exceeds \(2\) only \(10.8\%\) of the time.

Starting inside the cycle

To see the cycle attract from inside as well, the same computation was repeated from \((x, y) = (0, 0.5)\), which is inside the cycle for all five values of \(\mu\). The maxima of \(x\) now increase towards the same values until they reach them: for \(\mu = \tfrac14\) they are \(0.606543, 1.140036, 1.664822, 1.913297, 1.981599, \ldots\), reaching \(2.000637\) at the tenth maximum. For \(\mu = \tfrac{1}{16}\) the approach is again the slowest, with the tenth maximum still at \(1.692056\); for \(\mu = \tfrac12\) the tenth maximum is within \(2.0 \times 10^{-12}\) of the cycle, and for \(\mu = 1\) and \(4\) the solution reaches the cycle to rounding level within \(0 \le t \le 60\), as from outside.

Implementation Notes

  • Step size. The fixed-step method spends the same effort on the slow and the fast parts of a relaxation oscillation. For large \(\mu\) an adaptive method such as the Runge-Kutta-Fehlberg method puts small steps only where the solution changes quickly.
  • Interval length. How long to integrate depends on \(\mu\): the gap to the cycle shrinks by the factor \(\rho\) per turn, which is \(0.675\) for \(\mu = \tfrac{1}{16}\) but \(8.6 \times 10^{-4}\) for \(\mu = 1\).
  • End of the interval. A right end written to a few decimals, such as \(6.283185\) for \(2\pi\), shifts the exact end point; compare a computed value with the exact solution at the same \(t\), as in Example 1.
  • Symmetry check. The system (2) is unchanged when \((x, y)\) is replaced by \((-x, -y)\), so the computed cycle should be symmetric about the origin; its largest and smallest \(x\) agree to all the digits computed (\(\pm 2.022963\) for \(\mu = 4\)).
