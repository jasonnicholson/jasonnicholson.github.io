Galerkin’s Method

← Numerical Methods

Source inspiration: (Mathew 2000-2019).

Description

Galerkin’s method finds an approximate solution of a linear differential equation \(L[y(x)] + f(x) = 0\) on an interval \([a, b]\), where \(L\) is a linear differential operator such as \(L[y] = y'' + y\), together with an initial condition or two boundary conditions. Instead of looking for \(y\) among all functions, it looks only among the trial functions

\[ u(x) = \phi_0(x) + \sum_{j=1}^{n} c_j\,\phi_j(x), \]

where \(\phi_0\) satisfies the initial or boundary conditions and the basis functions \(\phi_1, \ldots, \phi_n\) satisfy the homogeneous versions of them, so every trial function satisfies the conditions whatever the coefficients \(c_j\) are. A trial function cannot usually make the residual \(r(x) = L[u(x)] + f(x)\) zero everywhere, so Galerkin’s method asks for the next best thing: the residual must be orthogonal to every basis function,

\[ \int_a^b \phi_i(x)\,\bigl(L[u(x)] + f(x)\bigr)\,dx = 0, \qquad i = 1, 2, \ldots, n. \]

Because \(L\) is linear, these are \(n\) linear equations \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{B}\) in the \(n\) unknown coefficients, with \(a_{ij} = \int_a^b \phi_i\,L[\phi_j]\,dx\) and \(b_i = -\int_a^b \phi_i\,\bigl(L[\phi_0] + f\bigr)\,dx\). Solving them gives the Galerkin solution \(u(x)\). Mathews solves one initial value problem with the polynomial basis \(x, x^2, \ldots, x^7\), and four boundary value problems, two with polynomials that vanish at both ends and two with the sine functions \(\sin kx\). In every example below the Galerkin solution is compared with the exact solution; the largest errors range from about \(5 \times 10^{-9}\) to \(1.5 \times 10^{-2}\), depending on how well the chosen basis can represent the exact solution.

Case 1 — Polynomial basis for an initial value problem (Example 2), \(y' + 5y - 1 - x + \sin 2x = 0\), \(y(0) = 1\), \(u_n = 1 + c_1 x + \cdots + c_n x^n\), \(n = 1\) to \(16\) on \(0 \le x \le 3\)

Behavior: The Galerkin systems are solved in 512-bit arithmetic. The largest error falls at every step, from \(0.7296\) for \(n = 1\) through \(0.01478\) for Mathews’ \(n = 7\) to \(2.374 \times 10^{-7}\) for \(n = 16\), and the point where it occurs moves toward the initial point, from \(x = 0.668\) to \(x = 0.0327\). The right panel plots these largest errors against \(n\) (left axis) and the price paid for them, the condition number \(\operatorname{cond}_\infty(\mathbf{A}_n)\) (right axis). It grows from \(179\) at \(n = 2\) to \(1.169 \times 10^{28}\) at \(n = 16\), by a factor between \(65\) and \(86\) for each added basis function, and is \(3.316 \times 10^{11}\) at \(n = 7\), as Mathews computes. The orange diamonds, and the dashed orange curve in the middle panel, come from rounding \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) to double precision and solving with Julia’s LU solver. Up to \(n = 9\) the largest error of that solution agrees with the exact Galerkin solution’s to four significant figures, although \(\operatorname{cond}_\infty(\mathbf{A}_9) = 1.478 \times 10^{15}\). From \(n = 12\) on it no longer follows the Galerkin error down: it stays between \(4.4 \times 10^{-5}\) and \(1.8 \times 10^{-3}\), rising and falling from one \(n\) to the next. These double-precision values depend on the linear-algebra library; NumPy’s solver gives different values of the same size, \(1.4 \times 10^{-4}\) to \(6.4 \times 10^{-4}\) for \(n = 12\) to \(16\).

Case 3 — Sine basis (Example 4 (b)), \(y'' + 2y + 2x(x - \pi) = 0\), \(y(0) = 0\), \(y(\pi) = 1\), \(u_n = x/\pi + c_1 \sin x + \cdots + c_n \sin nx\), \(n = 1\) to \(15\) on \(0 \le x \le \pi\)

Behavior: The Galerkin matrix is diagonal, so adding \(\sin nx\) to the basis adds the coefficient \(c_n\) and leaves \(c_1, \ldots, c_{n-1}\) unchanged; the right panel gains one point per frame. Each \(c_k\) (blue dot) sits inside the circle of the Fourier sine coefficient of \(y - x/\pi\), computed separately by Simpson’s rule from the exact solution; the two agree to better than one part in \(10^{12}\), as the callout above Example 4 (b) shows they must. The coefficients alternate in sign and decay like \(1/k^3\): \(|c_k|\) is within \(2\%\) of \(4/(\pi k^3)\) for \(k \ge 11\). The slow decay comes from the right end. The function \(y - x/\pi\) vanishes at both ends and its second derivative is \(0\) at \(x = 0\) but \(-2\) at \(x = \pi\), and three integrations by parts of its Fourier coefficient then give \(c_k \approx -4(-1)^k/(\pi k^3)\) for large \(k\). The error is largest near \(x = \pi\): from \(n = 2\) on, the largest error is at a point between \(x = 2.81\) and \(3.09\) that moves toward \(\pi\) as \(n\) grows. It falls at every step, but slowly, from \(0.3397\) for \(n = 1\) through \(0.008702\) for Mathews’ \(n = 7\) to \(0.002034\) for \(n = 15\), roughly like \(1/n^2\): \(n^2\) times the largest error stays between \(0.40\) and \(0.46\) for \(n = 5\) to \(15\).

Inner Products

NoteDefinition — Inner product

Consider the vector space of real functions whose domain is the closed interval \([a, b]\). The inner product of two functions \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) is

\[ \langle f, g \rangle = \int_a^b f(x)\,g(x)\,dx. \tag{1} \]

The inner product is a continuous, infinite-dimensional analogue of the dot product of linear algebra. If \(\langle f, g \rangle = 0\), then \(f\) and \(g\) are said to be orthogonal on \([a, b]\). All the functions used here are assumed to be square-integrable, that is, \(\langle f, f \rangle = \int_a^b f(x)^2\,dx < \infty\).

Example 1 (a) — \(f(x) = x^2 - 3x + 3\) and \(g(x) = 2x^3 + x^2 + 5x - 5\) over \([0, 2]\)

Step 1 (multiply out):

\[ f(x)\,g(x) = 2x^5 - 5x^4 + 8x^3 - 17x^2 + 30x - 15. \]

Step 2 (antiderivative):

\[ P(x) = \frac{x^6}{3} - x^5 + 2x^4 - \frac{17x^3}{3} + 15x^2 - 15x. \]

Step 3 (evaluate):

\[ \boxed{\ \langle f, g \rangle = P(2) - P(0) = 6 - 0 = 6\ } \]

Verification: a symbolic integration of \(f g\) over \([0, 2]\) returns \(6\), as in Mathews’ output.

Example 1 (b) — the same \(f\) and \(g\) over \([-1, 1]\)

Over a symmetric interval the odd powers in \(f g\) integrate to zero, and only the even part \(-5x^4 - 17x^2 - 15\) contributes:

\[ \boxed{\ \langle f, g \rangle = \int_{-1}^{1} \bigl(-5x^4 - 17x^2 - 15\bigr)\,dx = 2\left(-1 - \frac{17}{3} - 15\right) = -\frac{130}{3} \approx -43.3333\ } \]

Verification: with \(P\) from Example 1 (a), \(P(1) - P(-1) = -\tfrac{13}{3} - 39 = -\tfrac{130}{3}\).

WarningMathews’ solution uses a different pair

Mathews states Example 1 (b) with the same \(f\) and \(g\) as part (a), but his solution notebook integrates a different pair over \([-1, 1]\): \(f(x) = 5x^3 - 3x^2 - 3x + 1\) and \(g(x) = 35x^4 - 30x^2 + 7x + 3\). That pair is orthogonal: the antiderivative of \(f g\) takes the value \(-\tfrac58\) at both \(x = 1\) and \(x = -1\), so \(\langle f, g \rangle = 0\), which is his printed answer and agrees with a symbolic integration. For the pair in the problem statement the inner product is \(-\tfrac{130}{3}\), as above.

Example 1 (c) — \(f(x) = \cos x + \sin 2x\) and \(g(x) = \sin 2x + \cos 3x\) over \([0, 2\pi]\)

Multiplying out gives four products. Over a full period \([0, 2\pi]\), \(\int_0^{2\pi} \cos x \sin 2x\,dx\), \(\int_0^{2\pi} \cos x \cos 3x\,dx\) and \(\int_0^{2\pi} \sin 2x \cos 3x\,dx\) are all \(0\), and only the square survives:

\[ \boxed{\ \langle f, g \rangle = \int_0^{2\pi} \sin^2 2x\,dx = \pi\ } \]

Verification: a symbolic integration of \(f g\) over \([0, 2\pi]\) returns \(\pi\), which is Mathews’ printed answer. (The step-by-step panel that follows it in his notebook works with \(g(x) = \cos 2x + \sin x\) instead, which is orthogonal to \(f\) on \([0, 2\pi]\) and gives \(0\).)

TipLemma

If \(f\) is continuous on \([a, b]\) and \(\langle w, f \rangle = 0\) for every function \(w\), then \(f(x) \equiv 0\) on \([a, b]\). (Take \(w = f\): then \(\int_a^b f(x)^2\,dx = 0\).)

Basis for a vector space

A complete basis for a vector space \(V\) of functions is a set \(S = \{\phi_i(x)\}_{i=0}^{\infty}\) of linearly independent functions such that every \(f \in V\) can be written uniquely as a linear combination

\[ f(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} c_j\,\phi_j(x). \]

For example, if \(V\) is the set of all polynomials and power series, a complete basis is \(S = \{\phi_i(x) = x^i\}_{i=0}^{\infty}\).

TipProperty (stated without proof)

If \(f \in V\) and \(\langle \phi_i, f \rangle = 0\) for all \(\phi_i \in S\), then \(f(x) \equiv 0\).

Mathews mentions the lemma and this property only as background. Together they motivate Galerkin’s method: a function orthogonal to every basis function is zero, so a residual orthogonal to the first \(n\) basis functions should be small.

Weighted Residual Methods

A weighted residual method uses a finite set of functions \(\{\phi_i(x)\}_{i=0}^{n}\). Consider the differential equation

\[ L[y(x)] + f(x) = 0 \quad \text{over } a \le x \le b, \tag{2} \]

where \(L[y(x)]\) denotes a linear differential operator. Multiplying (2) by an arbitrary weight function \(w(x)\) and integrating over \([a, b]\) gives

\[ \int_a^b w(x)\,\bigl(L[y(x)] + f(x)\bigr)\,dx = 0 \quad \text{for every } w(x). \tag{3} \]

Equations (2) and (3) are equivalent when \(L[y] + f\) is continuous: (2) gives (3) at once, and (3) for every \(w\) gives (2) by the lemma above. Now introduce a trial solution of the form

\[ u(x) = \phi_0(x) + \sum_{j=1}^{n} c_j\,\phi_j(x), \tag{4} \]

and replace \(y(x)\) with \(u(x)\) on the left side of (2). The residual is

\[ r(x) = L[u(x)] + f(x). \tag{5} \]

Unless \(y\) happens to be one of the trial functions, \(r(x)\) cannot be zero everywhere. The goal is to choose the coefficients so that \(u\) satisfies (3) at least for some choices of the weight function:

\[ \int_a^b w(x)\,\bigl(L[u(x)] + f(x)\bigr)\,dx = 0 \quad \text{for some choices of } w(x). \tag{6} \]

Galerkin’s Method

One of the most important weighted residual methods is due to the Russian mathematician Boris Grigoryevich Galerkin (1871–1945). Galerkin’s method takes the weight functions from the basis functions themselves, \(w(x) \in \{\phi_i(x)\}_{i=1}^{n}\).

ImportantGalerkin’s equations

Choose the coefficients \(c_1, \ldots, c_n\) of the trial solution (4) so that

\[ \int_a^b \phi_i(x)\,\bigl(L[u(x)] + f(x)\bigr)\,dx = 0, \qquad i = 1, 2, \ldots, n. \tag{7} \]

Substituting (4) and using the linearity of \(L\) turns (7) into

\[ \sum_{j=1}^{n} c_j \int_a^b \phi_i(x)\,L[\phi_j(x)]\,dx = -\int_a^b \phi_i(x)\,\bigl(L[\phi_0(x)] + f(x)\bigr)\,dx, \qquad i = 1, \ldots, n, \]

which is the linear system

\[ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{B}, \qquad a_{ij} = \langle \phi_i, L[\phi_j] \rangle, \qquad b_i = -\langle \phi_i, L[\phi_0] + f \rangle. \tag{8} \]

Mathews obtains the coefficients both ways: he solves the equations (7) directly, and he sets up the matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) and the vector \(\mathbf{B}\) of (8) and solves \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{B}\). Both give the same coefficients in every example.

Algorithm

  1. Write the equation as \(L[y] + f = 0\) and choose \(\phi_0\) to satisfy the initial or boundary conditions.
  2. Choose basis functions \(\phi_1, \ldots, \phi_n\) that satisfy the homogeneous conditions, so that every trial solution (4) satisfies the original ones.
  3. Form the residual \(r(x) = L[u(x)] + f(x)\) of the trial solution.
  4. Compute the integrals in (8) and solve \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{B}\) for \(c_1, \ldots, c_n\).
  5. The Galerkin solution is \(u(x) = \phi_0(x) + \sum_{j=1}^{n} c_j\,\phi_j(x)\).
WarningConditioning of the Galerkin system

The system (8) can be ill conditioned: its solution can be very sensitive to rounding errors in \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\). Mathews computes the condition number \(\operatorname{cond}(\mathbf{A}) = \lVert \mathbf{A} \rVert\,\lVert \mathbf{A}^{-1} \rVert\) for each example and uses the estimate

\[ \lVert \mathbf{c} - \hat{\mathbf{c}} \rVert \lesssim \lVert \mathbf{c} \rVert\,\operatorname{cond}(\mathbf{A})\,\epsilon \]

for the error of the computed solution \(\hat{\mathbf{c}}\) when the entries of \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are rounded to machine precision \(\epsilon = 2^{-52} \approx 2.22 \times 10^{-16}\). In words: the computed coefficients lose about \(\log_{10} \operatorname{cond}(\mathbf{A})\) of their sixteen or so decimal digits. This is a first-order estimate of the typical size of the error, not a guaranteed bound. Mathews’ condition numbers are in the maximum-row-sum (\(\infty\)-)norm, and the values quoted on this page are too.

Galerkin’s Method for an Initial Value Problem

Suppose we wish to solve the initial value problem

\[ L[y(x)] + f(x) = 0, \qquad y(a) = y_0, \qquad a \le x \le b. \tag{9} \]

Take \(\phi_0(x) = y_0\) and the trial function

\[ u(x) = y_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{n} c_j\,\phi_j(x), \tag{10} \]

with basis functions that vanish at the initial point, \(\phi_j(a) = 0\), so that \(u(a) = y_0\) for every choice of the coefficients. There are \(n\) equations to solve for \(\{c_i\}_{i=1}^{n}\):

\[ \int_a^b \phi_i(x)\,\Bigl(L\Bigl[\sum_{j=1}^{n} c_j\,\phi_j(x)\Bigr] + L[y_0] + f(x)\Bigr)\,dx = 0, \qquad i = 1, 2, \ldots, n. \tag{11} \]

Example 2 — \(y' + 5y - 1 - x + \sin 2x = 0\), \(y(0) = 1\), over \([0, 3]\)

WarningTwo versions of Example 2

Step 1 (operator and data): \(L[y] = y' + 5y\), \(f(x) = -1 - x + \sin 2x\), \(a = 0\), \(b = 3\), \(y_0 = 1\).

Step 2 (basis and trial solution): Mathews takes \(n = 7\) and the powers \(\phi_i(x) = x^i\), which all vanish at \(x = 0\):

\[ u(x) = 1 + c_1 x + c_2 x^2 + c_3 x^3 + c_4 x^4 + c_5 x^5 + c_6 x^6 + c_7 x^7. \]

Step 3 (residual):

\[ r(x) = u'(x) + 5u(x) - 1 - x + \sin 2x = 4 - x + \sin 2x + \sum_{j=1}^{7} c_j\,\bigl(j x^{j-1} + 5x^j\bigr). \]

Step 4 (Galerkin system): since \(L[\phi_0] = L[1] = 5\), the entries of (8) are

\[ a_{ij} = \int_0^3 x^i\bigl(j x^{j-1} + 5x^j\bigr)\,dx = \frac{j\,3^{i+j}}{i+j} + \frac{5 \cdot 3^{i+j+1}}{i+j+1}, \qquad b_i = -\int_0^3 x^i\,\bigl(4 - x + \sin 2x\bigr)\,dx. \]

For example \(a_{11} = \tfrac92 + 45 = 49.5\), \(a_{77} = \tfrac{14348907}{2} \approx 7.17445 \times 10^6\) and \(b_1 = -9 + \tfrac32\cos 6 - \tfrac14\sin 6 \approx -7.48989\). The entries of \(\mathbf{A}\) span more than five orders of magnitude, and \(\mathbf{A}\) is not symmetric (\(a_{12} = \tfrac{477}{4}\), \(a_{21} = \tfrac{441}{4}\)).

Step 5 (solve): the solution of the \(7 \times 7\) system, computed exactly and rounded to six significant figures, is

\[ \mathbf{c} = (-3.65784,\ 6.84171,\ -7.37284,\ 4.88613,\ -1.84964,\ 0.363990,\ -0.0289550), \]

which agrees with Mathews’ printed values. The Galerkin solution is

\[ \boxed{\ u(x) = 1 - 3.65784\,x + 6.84171\,x^2 - 7.37284\,x^3 + 4.88613\,x^4 - 1.84964\,x^5 + 0.363990\,x^6 - 0.0289550\,x^7\ } \]

Verification: the exact solution is

\[ y(x) = \frac{1}{725}\Bigl(116 + 559\,e^{-5x} + 145x + 50\cos 2x - 125\sin 2x\Bigr), \]

which satisfies the differential equation and \(y(0) = 1\) (checked symbolically). Substituting the exact coefficients back into (7) gives \(0\) for all seven integrals.

Galerkin’s Method for a Boundary Value Problem

Suppose we wish to solve a boundary value problem over \([a, b]\):

\[ L[y(x)] + f(x) = 0, \qquad y(a) = z_a, \quad y(b) = z_b. \tag{12} \]

Define \(\phi_0\) as the straight line between the boundary points,

\[ \phi_0(x) = z_a + (z_b - z_a)\,\frac{x - a}{b - a}, \tag{13} \]

and use the trial function

\[ u(x) = \phi_0(x) + \sum_{j=1}^{n} c_j\,\phi_j(x). \tag{14} \]

The basis functions must all vanish at both ends,

\[ \phi_i(a) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \phi_i(b) = 0, \qquad i = 1, 2, \ldots, n, \]

so that \(u(a) = z_a\) and \(u(b) = z_b\) for every choice of the coefficients. There are \(n\) equations to solve for \(\{c_i\}_{i=1}^{n}\):

\[ \int_a^b \phi_i(x)\,\Bigl(L\Bigl[\sum_{j=1}^{n} c_j\,\phi_j(x)\Bigr] + L[\phi_0(x)] + f(x)\Bigr)\,dx = 0, \qquad i = 1, 2, \ldots, n. \tag{15} \]

For an operator \(L[y] = y'' + q\,y\) with a constant \(q\), as in Examples 3 and 4, integrating by parts and using \(\phi_i(a) = \phi_i(b) = 0\) gives \(a_{ij} = \int_a^b \bigl(q\,\phi_i \phi_j - \phi_i' \phi_j'\bigr)\,dx\), so the matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) is symmetric in those examples.

Example 3 (a) — \(y'' + y + 2x(1 - x) = 0\), \(y(0) = 0\), \(y(1) = 0\)

Step 1 (operator and data): \(L[y] = y'' + y\), \(f(x) = 2x(1 - x)\), \([a, b] = [0, 1]\), \(z_a = z_b = 0\), so \(\phi_0(x) = 0\).

Step 2 (basis and trial solution): \(n = 3\), with \(\phi_1 = x(x - 1)\), \(\phi_2 = x^2(x - 1)^2\), \(\phi_3 = x^3(x - 1)^3\):

\[ u(x) = c_1\,x(x - 1) + c_2\,x^2(x - 1)^2 + c_3\,x^3(x - 1)^3. \]

Step 3 (Galerkin system): the integrals (15) give

\[ \begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac{3}{10} & \tfrac{5}{84} & -\tfrac{4}{315} \\[1mm] \tfrac{5}{84} & -\tfrac{11}{630} & \tfrac{61}{13860} \\[1mm] -\tfrac{4}{315} & \tfrac{61}{13860} & -\tfrac{73}{60060} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ c_3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac{1}{15} \\[1mm] -\tfrac{1}{70} \\[1mm] \tfrac{1}{315} \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 4 (solve):

\[ c_1 = -\frac{1370}{7397} \approx -0.185210, \qquad c_2 = \frac{50688}{273689} \approx 0.185203, \qquad c_3 = -\frac{132}{21053} \approx -0.00626989. \]

These are Mathews’ values. The Galerkin solution is

\[ \boxed{\ u(x) = -\frac{1370}{7397}\,x(x - 1) + \frac{50688}{273689}\,x^2(x - 1)^2 - \frac{132}{21053}\,x^3(x - 1)^3\ } \]

Verification: the exact solution is

\[ y(x) = 2\Bigl(-2 - x + x^2 + 2\cos x + 2\sin x\,\tan\tfrac12\Bigr), \]

which satisfies the equation and both boundary conditions (checked symbolically).

WarningWhy part (b) is less accurate: the basis

Every one of Mathews’ five basis functions has the form \(x(x - 1)\,p(x)\) with \(p(0) = p(1)\) (the factors \(p\) are \(1\), \(x(x-1)\), \(x(x-1)^2\), \(x^2(x-1)^2\) and \(x^3(x-1)^2\)). The derivative of \(x(x-1)p(x)\) is \(-p(0)\) at \(x = 0\) and \(p(1)\) at \(x = 1\), so every trial function satisfies \(u'(0) + u'(1) = 2\,\phi_0' = 1\). The exact solution has \(y'(0) + y'(1) \approx 0.915244\), so no choice of the coefficients can reproduce it. In part (a) the same restriction does no harm: there \(\phi_0 = 0\) and the exact solution, which is symmetric about \(x = \tfrac12\), has \(y'(0) + y'(1) = 0\). With the five functions \(x^i(x - 1)\), \(i = 1, \ldots, 5\) (all polynomials of degree at most \(6\) that vanish at \(0\) and \(1\)) in place of Mathews’ basis, the largest error on the same grid falls to \(2.07 \times 10^{-8}\).

Conditioning: \(\operatorname{cond}(\mathbf{A}) \approx 87277.4\) and \(\lVert \mathbf{c} \rVert_2 \approx 3.23372\), so the estimate is \(\lVert \mathbf{c} - \hat{\mathbf{c}} \rVert \lesssim 6.27 \times 10^{-11}\), a loss of about \(5\) digits.

Example 4 (a) — \(y'' + 2y + 2x(x - \pi) = 0\), \(y(0) = 0\), \(y(\pi) = 0\)

Step 1 (operator and data): \(L[y] = y'' + 2y\), \(f(x) = 2x(x - \pi)\), \([a, b] = [0, \pi]\), \(\phi_0(x) = 0\).

Step 2 (basis): Mathews takes \(n = 3\) and the odd sines \(\phi_1 = \sin x\), \(\phi_2 = \sin 3x\), \(\phi_3 = \sin 5x\), which vanish at \(0\) and \(\pi\):

\[ u(x) = c_1 \sin x + c_2 \sin 3x + c_3 \sin 5x. \]

Step 3 (Galerkin system): since \(L[\sin kx] = (2 - k^2)\sin kx\) and the functions \(\sin kx\) are orthogonal on \([0, \pi]\) with \(\int_0^\pi \sin^2 kx\,dx = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\), the matrix is diagonal, \(a_{kk} = (2 - k^2)\tfrac{\pi}{2}\). The right-hand side is \(b_k = -\int_0^\pi \sin kx \cdot 2x(x - \pi)\,dx = \tfrac{8}{k^3}\) for odd \(k\), and the equations are

\[ \frac{\pi}{2}\,c_1 = 8, \qquad -\frac{7\pi}{2}\,c_2 = \frac{8}{27}, \qquad -\frac{23\pi}{2}\,c_3 = \frac{8}{125}. \]

Step 4 (solve):

\[ c_1 = \frac{16}{\pi} \approx 5.09296, \qquad c_2 = -\frac{16}{189\pi} \approx -0.0269469, \qquad c_3 = -\frac{16}{2875\pi} \approx -0.00177146, \]

so

\[ \boxed{\ u(x) = \frac{16\sin x}{\pi} - \frac{16\sin 3x}{189\pi} - \frac{16\sin 5x}{2875\pi}\ } \]

These agree with Mathews’ values. For even \(k\) the right-hand side \(b_k\) is \(0\) (the forcing \(2x(x - \pi)\) is symmetric about \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\) and \(\sin kx\) is antisymmetric about it), so adding \(\sin 2x\) and \(\sin 4x\) to the basis would give them zero coefficients; that is why Mathews skips them.

Verification: the exact solution is

\[ y(x) = 1 + \pi x - x^2 - \cos\bigl(\sqrt2\,x\bigr) - \sin\bigl(\sqrt2\,x\bigr)\tan\frac{\pi}{\sqrt2}, \]

which satisfies the equation (checked symbolically) and both boundary conditions (checked to 40 digits).

TipSine basis: Galerkin coefficients are Fourier coefficients

In Example 4 the basis functions are eigenfunctions of \(L\) with the same boundary conditions: for any \(v\) with \(v(0) = v(\pi) = 0\), integrating by parts twice gives \(\langle \sin kx, L[v] \rangle = (2 - k^2)\langle \sin kx, v \rangle\). Applying this to \(v = y - \phi_0\) turns Galerkin’s equation for \(c_k\) into \((2 - k^2)\tfrac{\pi}{2}\,c_k = (2 - k^2)\langle \sin kx, y - \phi_0 \rangle\), so

\[ c_k = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\pi \bigl(y(x) - \phi_0(x)\bigr)\sin kx\,dx, \]

the Fourier sine coefficient of \(y - \phi_0\) (this needs \(2 - k^2 \ne 0\), which holds for every integer \(k\)). The Galerkin solution is the truncated Fourier sine series of the exact solution, plus \(\phi_0\). Numerical quadrature of the exact solutions of Examples 4 (a) and 4 (b) reproduces every coefficient on this page to ten significant figures.
