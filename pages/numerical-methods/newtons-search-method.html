Newton’s Search Method

← Numerical Methods

Source inspiration: (Mathew 2000-2019).

Description

Newton’s search method looks for a local minimum of a function \(f(\mathbf{X})\) of several variables, \(\mathbf{X} = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)\), whose first and second partial derivatives can be evaluated. At the current point \(\mathbf{P}_k\) it replaces \(f\) by its second-degree Taylor polynomial, which is built from the gradient \(\nabla f(\mathbf{P}_k)\) and the Hessian matrix \(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_k)\) of second partial derivatives, and moves to the point where the gradient of that quadratic is zero:

\[ \mathbf{P}_{k+1} = \mathbf{P}_k + \Delta\mathbf{P}_k, \qquad \mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_k)\,\Delta\mathbf{P}_k = -\nabla f(\mathbf{P}_k). \]

This is the Newton-Raphson method applied to the system \(\nabla f(\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{0}\), with the Hessian playing the part of the Jacobian. With one variable it reduces to \(p_{k+1} = p_k - f'(p_k)/f''(p_k)\). Near a minimum where the Hessian is positive definite the iteration converges quadratically, and a quadratic \(f\) with a positive definite Hessian is minimised in a single step. But the method only looks for a zero of the gradient, so it converges just as readily to a saddle point or a maximum, and from a poor starting point it can run away altogether. Mathews works in two variables, \(\mathbf{P}_k = (x_k, y_k)\), so every run below is a path of points on the surface \(z = f(x, y)\), and every one has been recomputed in 60-digit arithmetic.

Gradient and Hessian

NoteDefinition — Gradient

If \(f(x, y)\) has partial derivatives \(f_x\) and \(f_y\), its gradient is the vector function

\[ \nabla f(x, y) = \bigl(f_x(x, y),\ f_y(x, y)\bigr). \]

NoteDefinition — Jacobian matrix

If \(f_1(x, y)\) and \(f_2(x, y)\) have partial derivatives, their Jacobian matrix is

\[ \mathbf{J}(x, y) = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial y} \\[2mm] \dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial y} \end{pmatrix}. \]

NoteDefinition — Hessian matrix

If \(f(x, y)\) has partial derivatives up to order two, its Hessian matrix is

\[ \mathbf{H}(x, y) = \begin{pmatrix} f_{xx}(x, y) & f_{xy}(x, y) \\ f_{yx}(x, y) & f_{yy}(x, y) \end{pmatrix}. \]

TipLemma 1 and Lemma 2
  1. The Hessian of \(f\) is the Jacobian of the two components of its gradient, \(f_1 = f_x\) and \(f_2 = f_y\): \(\ \mathbf{H}(x, y) = \mathbf{J}\bigl(f_x, f_y\bigr)\).
  2. If the second partial derivatives of \(f\) are continuous, then \(f_{xy} = f_{yx}\) and the Hessian is symmetric.

Example 1 — Gradient and Hessian of \(f(x, y) = (x - y)/(x^2 + y^2 + 2)\) at \((-3, -2)\)

Step 1 (first derivatives): by the quotient rule,

\[ f_x = -\frac{x^2 - 2xy - y^2 - 2}{(x^2 + y^2 + 2)^2}, \qquad f_y = -\frac{x^2 + 2xy - y^2 + 2}{(x^2 + y^2 + 2)^2}. \]

At \((-3, -2)\) the denominator is \(15^2 = 225\), and

\[ \nabla f(-3, -2) = \left(\frac{9}{225},\ -\frac{19}{225}\right) = \left(\frac{1}{25},\ -\frac{19}{225}\right) \approx (0.04,\ -0.084444). \]

Step 2 (second derivatives):

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{xx} &= \frac{2\,(x^3 - 3x^2 y - 3x y^2 + y^3 - 6x + 2y)}{(x^2 + y^2 + 2)^3}, \\ f_{xy} &= \frac{2\,(x^3 + 3x^2 y - 3x y^2 - y^3 + 2x - 2y)}{(x^2 + y^2 + 2)^3}, \\ f_{yy} &= -\frac{2\,(x^3 - 3x^2 y - 3x y^2 + y^3 + 2x - 6y)}{(x^2 + y^2 + 2)^3}. \end{aligned} \]

Step 3 (Hessian at the point): the denominator is \(15^3 = 3375\) and the three numerators are \(138\), \(-78\) and \(-122\), so

\[ \boxed{\ \mathbf{H}(-3, -2) = \frac{1}{3375}\begin{pmatrix} 138 & -78 \\ -78 & -122 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac{46}{1125} & -\tfrac{26}{1125} \\[1mm] -\tfrac{26}{1125} & -\tfrac{122}{3375} \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 0.040889 & -0.023111 \\ -0.023111 & -0.036148 \end{pmatrix}\ } \]

The Hessian agrees with Mathews’ output. Verification: the derivatives above were checked symbolically against direct differentiation (the differences simplify to \(0\)). The Hessian here is indefinite: \(\det \mathbf{H}(-3, -2) = -1528/759375 < 0\), and its eigenvalues are \(-0.042550\) and \(0.047290\). Near \((-3, -2)\) the surface curves up in one direction and down in another, which matters in Example 2.

The Quadratic Taylor Polynomial

Expand \(f\) about the point \(\mathbf{P} = (p, q)\) and write \(\Delta p = x - p\), \(\Delta q = y - q\). In series notation the second-degree Taylor polynomial is

\[ Q(x, y) = f(p, q) + f_x(p, q)\,\Delta p + f_y(p, q)\,\Delta q + \tfrac12\Bigl(f_{xx}(p, q)\,\Delta p^2 + 2 f_{xy}(p, q)\,\Delta p\,\Delta q + f_{yy}(p, q)\,\Delta q^2\Bigr). \tag{1} \]

With \(\mathbf{X} = (x, y)\) and \(\Delta\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{X} - \mathbf{P} = (\Delta p, \Delta q)\), the same polynomial in vector and matrix notation is

\[ Q(\mathbf{X}) = f(\mathbf{P}) + \nabla f(\mathbf{P}) \cdot \Delta\mathbf{P} + \tfrac12\, \Delta\mathbf{P}\, \mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P})\, \Delta\mathbf{P}^{T}. \tag{2} \]

Form (2) holds unchanged for \(n\) variables, with \(\nabla f\) a vector of \(n\) first partial derivatives and \(\mathbf{H}\) the \(n \times n\) matrix of second partial derivatives.

Example 2 — Taylor polynomials of \(f(x, y) = (x - y)/(x^2 + y^2 + 2)\)

(a) Centre \(\mathbf{P} = (-3, -2)\). From Example 1, \(f(-3, -2) = -\tfrac{1}{15}\), so by (1)

\[ Q(x, y) = -\frac{1}{15} + \frac{x + 3}{25} - \frac{19\,(y + 2)}{225} + \frac{23\,(x + 3)^2}{1125} - \frac{26\,(x + 3)(y + 2)}{1125} - \frac{61\,(y + 2)^2}{3375}, \]

and expanding,

\[ \boxed{\ Q(x, y) = \frac{-481 + 393x + 69x^2 - 763y - 78xy - 61y^2}{3375}\ } \]

which is Mathews’ simplified form. Because \(\mathbf{H}(-3, -2)\) is indefinite, this quadratic is a saddle surface: it has no minimum. Its only stationary point is at \((-\tfrac{1791}{382}, -\tfrac{622}{191}) \approx (-4.688482, -3.256545)\), where \(f = -0.041401\), higher than \(f(-3, -2) = -0.066667\). A Newton step from \((-3, -2)\) would go there.

(b) Centre \(\mathbf{P} = (-0.3, 0.2)\), closer to the minimum. Here

\[ f(-0.3, 0.2) = -\frac{50}{213} \approx -0.234742, \qquad \nabla f(-0.3, 0.2) \approx (0.403359,\ -0.425401), \]

\[ \mathbf{H}(-0.3, 0.2) \approx \begin{pmatrix} 0.447659 & -0.195579 \\ -0.195579 & 0.380190 \end{pmatrix}, \]

and

\[ Q(x, y) \approx 0.010829 + 0.576773\,x - 0.560112\,y + 0.223830\,x^2 - 0.195579\,xy + 0.190095\,y^2 . \]

This Hessian is positive definite (eigenvalues \(0.215457\) and \(0.612392\)), so \(Q\) is a bowl. Its minimum is at \((-0.831690,\ 1.045402)\), with \(Q = -0.521790\) and \(f = -0.495985\) there. The true minimum of \(f\) is at \((-1, 1)\), where \(f = -\tfrac12\).

Verification: both expansions were recomputed symbolically from (2), and the stationary points of \(Q\) were found by solving \(\nabla Q = \mathbf{0}\) exactly. The two stationary points of \(f\) itself are \((-1, 1)\), a minimum with \(\mathbf{H} = \tfrac14 \mathbf{I}\), and \((1, -1)\), a maximum with \(f = \tfrac12\). As Mathews observes, when the centre is close to the minimum, the minimum of the quadratic is close to the minimum of the surface. That is the idea of Newton’s method, and the minimiser of \(Q\) in (b) is exactly the first Newton step \(\mathbf{P}_1\) of Example 6.

Newton’s Method for Finding a Minimum

Assume that the first and second partial derivatives of \(f\) are continuous near a minimum \(\mathbf{P}^*\) and that \(\mathbf{P}_0\) is close to it. The minimum of the quadratic approximation (2), centred at \(\mathbf{P}_0\), is where \(\nabla Q(\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{0}\). Since \(\mathbf{H}\) is symmetric,

\[ \nabla Q(\mathbf{X}) = \nabla f(\mathbf{P}_0) + \Delta\mathbf{P}\, \mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_0), \]

so setting \(\nabla Q(\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{0}\) gives \(\Delta\mathbf{P}\, \mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_0) = -\nabla f(\mathbf{P}_0)\). If \(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_0)\) is invertible,

\[ \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{P}_0 - \nabla f(\mathbf{P}_0)\, \bigl(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_0)\bigr)^{-1}. \]

This \(\mathbf{X}\) is the next approximation \(\mathbf{P}_1\). Repeating the construction at each new point gives Newton’s method for finding a minimum:

ImportantNewton search formula

\[ \mathbf{P}_{k+1} = \mathbf{P}_k - \nabla f(\mathbf{P}_k)\, \bigl(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_k)\bigr)^{-1} \qquad \text{(row vectors)}, \tag{3} \]

\[ \mathbf{P}_{k+1}^{T} = \mathbf{P}_k^{T} - \bigl(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_k)\bigr)^{-1} \nabla f(\mathbf{P}_k)^{T} \qquad \text{(column vectors)}. \tag{4} \]

The two forms agree because \(\mathbf{H}\) is symmetric. Form (4) is the Newton-Raphson formula \(\mathbf{P}_{k+1}^{T} = \mathbf{P}_k^{T} - \mathbf{J}(\mathbf{P}_k)^{-1} \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{P}_k)^{T}\) for a root of the vector function \(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X}) = \nabla f(\mathbf{X})\), since by Lemma 1 its Jacobian is \(\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{H}\). For a function of one variable, (3) is \(p_{k+1} = p_k - f'(p_k)/f''(p_k)\): Newton-Raphson applied to \(f'(x) = 0\).

The theory of Newton-Raphson therefore carries over. The following is a general result, not a claim about any one example:

TipTheorem — Local quadratic convergence (general)

Suppose the second partial derivatives of \(f\) are continuous near \(\mathbf{P}^*\) and satisfy a Lipschitz condition there, that \(\nabla f(\mathbf{P}^*) = \mathbf{0}\), and that \(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}^*)\) is nonsingular. Then there is a \(\delta > 0\) such that for every \(\mathbf{P}_0\) with \(\lVert \mathbf{P}_0 - \mathbf{P}^* \rVert < \delta\) the iterates (3) are defined, converge to \(\mathbf{P}^*\), and satisfy

\[ \lVert \mathbf{P}_{k+1} - \mathbf{P}^* \rVert \le C\, \lVert \mathbf{P}_k - \mathbf{P}^* \rVert^2 \]

for some constant \(C\). The point \(\mathbf{P}^*\) is a strict local minimum if \(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}^*)\) is positive definite, a strict local maximum if it is negative definite, and a saddle point if it is indefinite.

The theorem does not require \(\mathbf{P}^*\) to be a minimum. Newton’s method treats every nondegenerate stationary point alike. Examples 5 and 6 show it converging quadratically to two saddle points and to a maximum, so the type of the limit has to be checked afterwards with the Hessian. Nor does the theorem say how small \(\delta\) is: Example 6 shows a starting point from which the iterates go off to infinity.

Algorithm

ImportantAlgorithm — Newton’s method for finding a minimum

Start with an approximation \(\mathbf{P}_0\) to the minimum point \(\mathbf{P}^*\). Set \(k = 0\).

  1. Evaluate the gradient vector \(\nabla f(\mathbf{P}_k)\) and the Hessian matrix \(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_k)\).
  2. Solve the linear system \(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_k)\, \Delta\mathbf{P}_k^{T} = -\nabla f(\mathbf{P}_k)^{T}\) and set \(\mathbf{P}_{k+1} = \mathbf{P}_k + \Delta\mathbf{P}_k\).
  3. Perform the termination test: stop when \(\lVert \Delta\mathbf{P}_k \rVert\) and \(\lVert \nabla f(\mathbf{P}_{k+1}) \rVert\) are small enough. Otherwise set \(k = k + 1\) and repeat.
  4. Check the Hessian at the final point. If it is not positive definite, the point is not a local minimum.

Mathews’ formula (3) is written with the inverse of the Hessian, but as he notes, it is better to solve the linear system in step 2 with a linear system solver: the inverse is a theoretical tool, and computing it is inefficient. His program NewtonSearch runs a fixed number of iterations of (3) and prints each point and the value of \(f\) there; the tables below do the same.

Example 3 — A quadratic function: one step is exact

Use Newton’s method to find the minimum of

\[ f(x, y) = x^2 - 4x + y^2 - y - xy \]

starting from \(\mathbf{P}_0 = (0, 0)\).

Step 1: the gradient and Hessian are

\[ \nabla f(x, y) = (2x - y - 4,\ 2y - x - 1), \qquad \mathbf{H} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. \]

The Hessian is constant, with eigenvalues \(1\) and \(3\), so it is positive definite everywhere.

Step 2: at \(\mathbf{P}_0\), \(\nabla f(0, 0) = (-4, -1)\). Solve

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \Delta x \\ \Delta y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta\mathbf{P}_0 = (3,\ 2). \]

Step 3: \(\mathbf{P}_1 = (0, 0) + (3, 2) = (3, 2)\), and \(\nabla f(3, 2) = (6 - 2 - 4,\ 4 - 3 - 1) = (0, 0)\).

\(k\) \(x_k\) \(y_k\) \(f(x_k, y_k)\) \(\lVert \mathbf{P}_k - \mathbf{P}^* \rVert\) \(\lVert \nabla f(\mathbf{P}_k) \rVert\)
0 0.000000000000 0.000000000000 0 3.61 4.12
1 3.000000000000 2.000000000000 -7.000000000000 0 0
2 3.000000000000 2.000000000000 -7.000000000000 0 0

\[ \boxed{\ \mathbf{P}^* = (3, 2), \qquad f(3, 2) = -7\ } \]

Mathews asks whether the result is surprising: the method converged in one iteration. It should, because the quadratic approximation of a quadratic surface is the surface itself, so the minimum of \(Q\) is the minimum of \(f\). Later iterations stay at \((3, 2)\).

Verification: \(\nabla f(3, 2) = (0, 0)\) and the Hessian is positive definite, so \((3, 2)\) is the minimum. Because \(\mathbf{H}\) is positive definite everywhere, \(f\) is convex and this is the global minimum.

Example 4 — Rosenbrock’s parabolic valley

Use Newton’s method to find the minimum of Rosenbrock’s function (circa 1960)

\[ f(x, y) = 100\,(y - x^2)^2 + (1 - x)^2 \]

starting from \(\mathbf{P}_0 = (0.99, 1.01)\). The minimum is at \((1, 1)\), where \(f = 0\). It lies in a long, curved valley along the parabola \(y = x^2\).

Step 1: the gradient and Hessian are

\[ \nabla f = \bigl(-400x(y - x^2) - 2(1 - x),\ \ 200(y - x^2)\bigr), \qquad \mathbf{H} = \begin{pmatrix} 1200x^2 - 400y + 2 & -400x \\ -400x & 200 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 2: at \(\mathbf{P}_0 = (0.99, 1.01)\), \(y - x^2 = 0.0299\) and \(f(\mathbf{P}_0) = 0.089501\), so

\[ \nabla f(\mathbf{P}_0) = (-11.8604,\ 5.98), \qquad \mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_0) = \begin{pmatrix} 774.12 & -396 \\ -396 & 200 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 3: solving \(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_0)\,\Delta\mathbf{P}_0^T = -\nabla f(\mathbf{P}_0)^T\) gives \(\Delta\mathbf{P}_0 = \bigl(-\tfrac{1}{498},\ -\tfrac{28117}{830000}\bigr) \approx (-0.002008,\ -0.033876)\), so

\[ \mathbf{P}_1 \approx (0.987992,\ 0.976124), \qquad f(\mathbf{P}_1) = 1.441945 \times 10^{-4}. \]

Several things happen here:

  • The Hessian at \(\mathbf{P}_0\) is not positive definite: \(\det \mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_0) = -1992\), with eigenvalues \(-2.04\) and \(976.16\). The first step still lowers \(f\), and it lands almost exactly on the valley floor (\(y_1 - x_1^2 = -4.0 \times 10^{-6}\), against \(0.0299\) at \(\mathbf{P}_0\)), but it moves away from \((1, 1)\): the distance grows from \(0.0141\) to \(0.0267\). From \(k = 1\) on, the Hessian is positive definite at every iterate.
  • From then on the convergence is quadratic: the ratio \(\lVert \mathbf{P}_{k+1} - \mathbf{P}^* \rVert / \lVert \mathbf{P}_k - \mathbf{P}^* \rVert^2\) alternates between about \(0.23\) and about \(23\) instead of settling to one value, but it stays bounded, and the number of correct digits roughly doubles each step (\(1.6 \times 10^{-4}\), \(6.1 \times 10^{-7}\), \(8.3 \times 10^{-14}\), \(1.6 \times 10^{-25}\)).
  • The function values in the table are computed in 60-digit arithmetic. A double-precision run gives the same points to the printed 12 decimals, but its last values of \(f\) are rounding noise. Mathews’ printout shows \(f = 5.40 \times 10^{-25}\) at \(k = 4\) and \(3.16 \times 10^{-30}\) at \(k = 5\); a double-precision rerun gives \(5.40 \times 10^{-25}\) and \(2.7 \times 10^{-29}\), while the exact values are \(5.38 \times 10^{-25}\) and \(4.9 \times 10^{-51}\).

Mathews compares the answer with Mathematica’s FindMinimum started at \((1.8, 1.2)\) and remarks that the function is “flat” near the minimum. In numbers: \(\mathbf{H}(1, 1) = \begin{pmatrix} 802 & -400 \\ -400 & 200 \end{pmatrix}\) has eigenvalues \(0.3994\) and \(1001.6\), so along the valley \(f\) rises about \(2500\) times more slowly than across it.

Example 5 — Three minima and two saddle points

Use Newton’s method to find the minima of

\[ f(x, y) = 5x^2 + x^4 - 9x^2 y + 3y^2 + 2y^4 + \tfrac14 x . \]

Mathews asks the reader to estimate the local minima from graphs of the surface and its contours on \(-3 \le x \le 3\), \(-0.5 \le y \le 2\), and then to plot the curves where each component of the gradient is zero:

\[ \nabla f = \bigl(4x^3 + 10x - 18xy + \tfrac14,\ \ -9x^2 + 6y + 8y^3\bigr), \qquad \mathbf{H} = \begin{pmatrix} 12x^2 - 18y + 10 & -18x \\ -18x & 24y^2 + 6 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Convergence to a maximum

Because \(f(-x, -y) = -f(x, y)\), the gradient satisfies \(\nabla f(-\mathbf{X}) = \nabla f(\mathbf{X})\) and the Hessian \(\mathbf{H}(-\mathbf{X}) = -\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{X})\), so the Newton step from \(-\mathbf{P}\) is exactly the negative of the step from \(\mathbf{P}\). Started at \((0.3, -0.2)\), Newton’s method therefore produces the negatives of the iterates in the table above, \((0.831690, -1.045402)\), \((0.988071, -0.993270)\), \(\ldots\), and converges just as quickly to \((1, -1)\), where \(f = \tfrac12\) is the maximum (\(\mathbf{H}(1, -1) = -\tfrac14 \mathbf{I}\)). In one variable the same holds in general: by the theorem with \(n = 1\), \(p_{k+1} = p_k - f'(p_k)/f''(p_k)\) started close enough to a maximum, where \(f'' < 0\), converges to that maximum.

Enhanced Newton Method for Finding a Minimum

Newton’s method can be modified by treating \(\mathbf{S}_k = -\nabla f(\mathbf{P}_k)\,\bigl(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_k)\bigr)^{-1}\) as a search direction, as the gradient method uses the direction \(-\nabla f(\mathbf{P}_k)\), and doing a one-parameter minimisation (line search) along it:

  1. Evaluate the gradient vector \(\nabla f(\mathbf{P}_k)\) and the Hessian matrix \(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_k)\).
  2. Compute the search direction \(\mathbf{S}_k = -\nabla f(\mathbf{P}_k)\,\bigl(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_k)\bigr)^{-1}\).
  3. Minimise the function of one variable \(\phi(t) = f(\mathbf{P}_k + t\,\mathbf{S}_k)\), for instance with a golden ratio or quadratic interpolative search, giving a value \(t_k\). This is a minimum of \(f\) along the search line; at an interior minimum \(\phi'(t_k) = \nabla f(\mathbf{P}_k + t_k\,\mathbf{S}_k) \cdot \mathbf{S}_k = 0\), so the gradient at the new point is perpendicular to the line.
  4. Set \(\mathbf{P}_{k+1} = \mathbf{P}_k + t_k\,\mathbf{S}_k\).
  5. Perform the termination test: are \(f(\mathbf{P}_k)\) and \(f(\mathbf{P}_{k+1})\) sufficiently close, and is \(\lVert \mathbf{P}_{k+1} - \mathbf{P}_k \rVert\) small enough? If not, set \(k = k + 1\) and repeat.

With \(t_k = 1\) this is the plain method (3). In general, Mathews says, the modified method is more reliable, and he leaves its investigation to the reader. Two of the runs above show what the line search can and cannot fix:

  • In the Example 4 variant from \((1.8, 1.2)\), the direction at \(k = 1\) points downhill but the full step overshoots and raises \(f\) from \(0.637\) to \(40.4\). A line search along that direction would pick a step that lowers \(f\).
  • From \((-3, 2)\) in Example 6, \(\mathbf{S}_k\) points uphill at every iterate. If \(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_k)\) is positive definite, then \(\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{S}_k = -\nabla f\, \mathbf{H}^{-1} \nabla f^{T} < 0\) and \(\mathbf{S}_k\) is a descent direction, but here \(\mathbf{H}\) is indefinite. A line search over \(t > 0\) along an uphill direction cannot lower \(f\). The direction itself has to be changed, for example to \(-\nabla f(\mathbf{P}_k)\) or to the Newton direction of a modified, positive definite Hessian.

Implementation Notes

  • Solve, don’t invert. Compute \(\Delta\mathbf{P}_k\) from \(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}_k)\,\Delta\mathbf{P}_k^T = -\nabla f(\mathbf{P}_k)^T\) with a linear solver (for example a Cholesky factorisation, which also reports whether \(\mathbf{H}\) is positive definite).
  • Cost per step. Each iteration needs the \(n\) first partial derivatives, the \(n(n+1)/2\) distinct second partial derivatives and the solution of an \(n \times n\) system. The gradient method needs only the first derivatives but generally converges much more slowly.
  • Check what was found. The iteration stops where \(\nabla f = \mathbf{0}\), and that can be a saddle point (Example 5) or a maximum (Example 6). Test the Hessian at the final point.
  • Watch the starting point. Convergence is guaranteed only from close enough to \(\mathbf{P}^*\). Signs of trouble are a Hessian that is not positive definite and a step that increases \(f\) (Example 6 from \((-3, 2)\)). A safeguarded version takes a line search along the Newton direction, and falls back to \(-\nabla f\) when that direction is not downhill.
  • Termination. Use \(\lVert \nabla f(\mathbf{P}_k) \rVert\) and \(\lVert \Delta\mathbf{P}_k \rVert\), not only the change in \(f\). Near a minimum \(f(\mathbf{P}) - f(\mathbf{P}^*) \approx \tfrac12 (\mathbf{P} - \mathbf{P}^*)\,\mathbf{H}\,(\mathbf{P} - \mathbf{P}^*)^T\) is quadratic in the error, so the values of \(f\) settle long before the point does. In Example 6 the printed \(f(\mathbf{P}_4)\) is already \(-0.500000000000\) while \(\mathbf{P}_4\) is still \(3.7 \times 10^{-8}\) from \((-1, 1)\).
  • Flat valleys. When the Hessian at the minimum is badly conditioned (eigenvalues \(0.40\) and \(1002\) for Rosenbrock’s function), function values alone locate the minimum poorly along the valley. Newton’s method still converges quickly there, because it uses the curvature in each direction.
