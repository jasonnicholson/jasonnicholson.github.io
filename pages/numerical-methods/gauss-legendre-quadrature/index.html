Gauss-Legendre Quadrature

← Numerical Methods

Source inspiration: (Mathew 2000-2019).

Description

Gauss-Legendre quadrature approximates \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) from \(n\) values of \(f\). Unlike the equally spaced Newton-Cotes rules, it chooses both the nodes and the weights, so that the rule is exact for polynomials of the highest possible degree. On \([-1,1]\) the \(n\)-point rule is \[ \int_{-1}^{1} g(t)\,dt \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} \omega_i\,g(t_i), \qquad \omega_i=\frac{2}{(1-t_i^2)\,[P_n'(t_i)]^2}, \] where the nodes \(t_i\) are the \(n\) roots of the Legendre polynomial \(P_n(t)\). The roots all lie inside \((-1,1)\) and the weights \(\omega_i\) are positive. For a general interval \([a,b]\), the change of variable \(x=\tfrac{a+b}{2}+\tfrac{b-a}{2}t\) gives the rule the animation draws: \[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx I_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\,f(x_i), \qquad x_i=\frac{a+b}{2}+\frac{b-a}{2}\,t_i, \qquad w_i=\frac{b-a}{2}\,\omega_i . \]

The rule needs only a finite interval and the values of \(f\) at the interior nodes; it never evaluates \(f\) at \(a\) or \(b\). Its error formula needs much more: \(f\) must have \(2n\) continuous derivatives, \(f\in C^{2n}[a,b]\).

As a general result, the \(n\)-point rule is exact for every polynomial of degree at most \(2n-1\), and no rule with \(n\) nodes is exact for all polynomials of degree \(2n\). If \(f\in C^{2n}[a,b]\), there is a \(c\) with \(a<c<b\) such that \[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx - I_n = \frac{(b-a)^{2n+1}\,(n!)^4}{(2n+1)\,[(2n)!]^3}\,f^{(2n)}(c), \] which for \(n=2\) on \([-1,1]\) is Mathews’ error term \(f^{(4)}(c)/135\). For an \(f\) that is analytic on a neighbourhood of \([a,b]\), the error falls at least geometrically as \(n\) grows; Case 1 quotes this for the smooth integrand \(1+e^{-x}\sin 8x\).

Case 1 itself shows what happens when the smoothness hypothesis fails. Its integrand \(f(x)=1+e^{-x}\sin(8x^{2/3})\) has \(f'\) unbounded at \(x=0\), so \(f\) is not even in \(C^1[0,2]\), and the error shrinks only algebraically: in the table below, doubling \(n\) divides it by about 9.2, 9.6 and then 9.8, approaching \(2^{10/3}\approx 10.08\). Few nodes can also miss the shape of \(f\). At \(n=2\) the nodes \(x\approx0.423\) and \(1.577\) sit near the two troughs of \(f\), and \(I_2=1.154628\) is about 43% low. Nor does the error always fall as \(n\) grows: \(|I_5-I|\approx1.7\times10^{-3}\), but \(|I_6-I|\approx3.2\times10^{-3}\).

Gauss-Legendre quadrature gains accuracy by raising \(n\) on the whole interval, while the Trapezoidal Rule and Simpson’s Rule gain it by shrinking \(h\), which Romberg Integration speeds up by extrapolation and Adaptive Simpson’s Rule concentrates where \(f\) needs it.

Case 1 — Increasing-Order Gauss-Legendre Rule, \(f(x)=1+e^{-x}\sin(8x^{2/3})\) on \([0,2]\)

Behavior: Each shaded bar has width \(w_i\) and height \(f(x_i)\), marked by a red dot at the node \(x_i\), so its area is the term \(w_i f(x_i)\) of the sum. The bars are laid end to end across \([0,2]\), and each node lies inside its own bar. As \(n\) grows, the nodes become denser and the estimate settles toward \(I\approx2.016280\): from \(n=6\) on it decreases in every frame, and the last frame (\(n=64\)) gives 2.016282, still about \(1.9\times10^{-6}\) high. The \(x^{2/3}\) term makes \(f'\) unbounded at \(x=0\) (\(f'(x)\approx \tfrac{16}{3}x^{-1/3}\) near \(0\)), so the textbook error order, which assumes smooth \(f\), does not hold for this integrand. The measured error shrinks only algebraically, like \(O(n^{-10/3})\): doubling \(n\) divides it by about 9.8 (from 32 to 64 nodes), tending to \(2^{10/3}\approx 10.08\). For contrast, the smooth integrand \(1+e^{-x}\sin 8x\) on \([0,2]\) already has error about \(1.8\times10^{-13}\) at \(n=14\) and reaches double-precision round-off level (about \(10^{-15}\) or less) by \(n=16\).
