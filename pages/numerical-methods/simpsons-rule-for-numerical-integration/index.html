Simpson’s Rule for Numerical Integration

← Numerical Methods

Source inspiration: (Mathew 2000-2019).

Description

Simpson’s rule approximates a definite integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) from values of \(f\) at equally spaced points. It splits \([a,b]\) into \(n\) subintervals of width \(h=(b-a)/n\), with nodes \(x_k=a+kh\) for \(k=0,1,\dots,n\), and on each pair of subintervals \([x_{2k-2},x_{2k}]\) it replaces \(f\) by the parabola through the three nodes. Adding the areas under the parabolas gives the composite rule \[ S_n = \frac{h}{3}\left[f(x_0)+f(x_n)+4\sum_{k=1}^{n/2} f(x_{2k-1}) + 2\sum_{k=1}^{n/2-1} f(x_{2k})\right]. \]

Each parabola spans two subintervals, so \(n\) must be even. The sums \(S_n\) converge to the integral for any continuous \(f\), but the error formula below needs more: \(f\) must be four times continuously differentiable on \([a,b]\), \(f\in C^4[a,b]\).

Although each panel uses a parabola, the rule is exact for every cubic, because the cubic term’s error cancels by symmetry about the panel’s midpoint; the first term it misses involves \(f^{(4)}\). The general result, as Mathews states it: if \(f\in C^4[a,b]\), there is a \(c\) with \(a<c<b\) such that \[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx - S_n = -\frac{(b-a)\,f^{(4)}(c)}{180}\,h^4 . \] The error is therefore \(O(h^4)\), and halving \(h\) typically divides it by about 16. The animation on this page does not show this rate, because its integrand is not in \(C^4[0,2]\).

Case 1 shows what happens when that hypothesis fails. Its integrand \(f(x)=e^{-x}\sin(8x^{2/3})+1\) has \(f'\) unbounded at \(x=0\), and the error shrinks only like \(h^{5/3}\): in the table below, halving \(h\) divides it by 3.38 and then 3.22, approaching \(2^{5/3}\approx 3.17\), not 16. The Trapezoidal Rule’s error on the same integrand also shrinks like \(h^{5/3}\), so Simpson’s rule keeps only a constant-factor advantage, an error about 3.5 times smaller for \(n\) from 48 to 108. On the coarsest frames (\(n\le 6\)) its error is larger than the trapezoidal rule’s.

\(n\) \(h\) \(S_n\) \(S_n-I\) ratio
6 \(1/3\) 1.840560 \(-0.1757\)
12 \(1/6\) 1.991249 \(-0.02503\) 7.02
24 \(1/12\) 2.010370 \(-0.005910\) 4.24
48 \(1/24\) 2.014531 \(-0.001748\) 3.38
96 \(1/48\) 2.015737 \(-0.0005427\) 3.22

The rows are frames of Case 1, each halving \(h\), against \(I=\int_0^2 f(x)\,dx\approx 2.016280\); the ratio is the previous row’s error divided by this row’s.

Simpson’s rule is the degree-2 member of the Newton-Cotes family, between the trapezoidal rule and the higher-degree Simpson’s 3/8 Rule and Boole’s Rule; with \(T_n\) the trapezoidal sum, \(S_{2n}=(4T_{2n}-T_n)/3\) is one Richardson step and forms the second column of Romberg Integration, and Adaptive Simpson’s Rule applies the rule panel by panel.

Case 1 — Composite Simpson’s Rule, \(f(x)=e^{-x}\sin(8x^{2/3})+1\) on \([0,2]\)

Behavior: Quadratic panels are rebuilt over the full interval as sample density increases, and the approximation converges toward the true integral \(\int_0^2 f(x)\,dx \approx 2.016280\); the last frame (109 sample points) gives 2.015834, about 0.00045 low. The \(x^{2/3}\) term makes \(f'\) unbounded at \(x=0\) (\(f'(x)\approx \tfrac{16}{3}x^{-1/3}\) near \(0\)), so the textbook error order, which assumes smooth \(f\), does not hold for this integrand. The measured error shrinks like \(O(h^{5/3})\) rather than \(O(h^4)\): halving \(h\) divides it by about 3.38 (from 24 to 48 subintervals), tending to \(2^{5/3}\approx 3.17\), instead of 16.
