Faddeev-Leverrier Method

← Numerical Methods

Source inspiration: (Mathew 2000-2019).

The eigenvalues of an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\) are the roots of its characteristic polynomial (see Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors). Expanding \(\det(\lambda I - A)\) by cofactors gives a sum of \(n!\) terms, which is impractical beyond small \(n\). The Faddeev-Leverrier method finds every coefficient of the characteristic polynomial from a short recursion that uses only matrix products and traces. The same recursion also produces the inverse \(A^{-1}\) and the determinant \(\det A\) with almost no extra work.

The method is named after Urbain Le Verrier, who obtained the coefficients from the traces of the powers \(A^k\) through Newton’s identities, and D. K. Faddeev, who recast the computation as the matrix recursion below. It is an exact, finite algorithm and is well suited to hand computation and computer algebra. As a floating-point method for eigenvalues it is a poor choice for all but small matrices; the last section shows why.

The Method

NoteDefinition — Trace and Characteristic Polynomial

The trace of an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A = (a_{ij})\) is the sum of its diagonal entries, \[ \operatorname{tr}(A) = a_{11} + a_{22} + \cdots + a_{nn}. \] On this page the characteristic polynomial is the monic polynomial \[ P(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A) = \lambda^n - p_1\lambda^{n-1} - p_2\lambda^{n-2} - \cdots - p_{n-1}\lambda - p_n . \tag{1} \] The eigenvalues of \(A\) are the roots of \(P(\lambda) = 0\).

Mathews first writes the polynomial as \(P(\lambda) = \lambda^n + c_1\lambda^{n-1} + \cdots + c_n\), then, like this page, as (1), so \(c_k = -p_k\). The minus signs in (1) make the recursion below free of signs.

ImportantAlgorithm — Faddeev-Leverrier

Start with \[ B_1 = A, \qquad p_1 = \operatorname{tr}(B_1). \tag{2} \] For \(k = 2, 3, \ldots, n\) compute \[ B_k = A\,(B_{k-1} - p_{k-1} I), \qquad p_k = \frac{1}{k}\operatorname{tr}(B_k). \tag{3} \] Then \(p_1, \ldots, p_n\) are the coefficients in (1). If \(p_n \neq 0\), the inverse of \(A\) is \[ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{p_n}\,(B_{n-1} - p_{n-1} I). \tag{4} \]

TipTheorem — What the Recursion Produces

Let \(A\) be any \(n\times n\) matrix and let \(B_k\), \(p_k\) be defined by (2)–(3). Then

  1. \(P(\lambda) = \lambda^n - p_1\lambda^{n-1} - \cdots - p_n\) is the characteristic polynomial of \(A\);
  2. \(B_n = p_n I\), which is the Cayley–Hamilton theorem \(P(A) = 0\) in disguise;
  3. \(\det A = (-1)^{n+1} p_n\), so \(A\) is nonsingular exactly when \(p_n \neq 0\);
  4. if \(p_n \neq 0\), then \(A^{-1}\) is given by (4).

The last product \(B_n\) is needed only for \(p_n\), and the matrix \(B_{n-1} - p_{n-1}I\) in (4) has already been formed to compute it. So the inverse costs one extra scaling. If the whole of \(B_n\) is formed, the identity \(B_n = p_n I\) is a useful check on the arithmetic.

Why the Recursion Works

Mathews refers to V. N. Faddeeva’s Computational Methods of Linear Algebra (Dover, 1959) for the proof. Here is a short version.

Write \(C_k = B_k - p_k I\) for \(k = 1, \ldots, n\) and \(C_0 = I\), so that (3) reads \(B_k = A\,C_{k-1}\). The adjugate (classical adjoint) of \(\lambda I - A\) is a matrix polynomial of degree \(n-1\) in \(\lambda\), and \[ (\lambda I - A)\,\operatorname{adj}(\lambda I - A) = P(\lambda)\, I . \] Write \(\operatorname{adj}(\lambda I - A) = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \lambda^{n-1-k} D_k\) and \(P(\lambda) = \lambda^n - \sum_{k=1}^{n} q_k \lambda^{n-k}\) with, for the moment, unknown \(q_k\). Matching powers of \(\lambda\) gives \[ D_0 = I, \qquad D_k = A D_{k-1} - q_k I \quad (1 \le k \le n-1), \qquad A D_{n-1} = q_n I . \tag{5} \] To find \(q_k\), use Jacobi’s formula: the derivative of a determinant is the trace of the adjugate times the derivative of the matrix, so \(P'(\lambda) = \operatorname{tr}\operatorname{adj}(\lambda I - A)\). Comparing the coefficients of \(\lambda^{n-1-k}\) on both sides gives \[ \operatorname{tr}(D_k) = -(n-k)\,q_k \quad (1 \le k \le n-1). \] Since \(\operatorname{tr}(D_k) = \operatorname{tr}(A D_{k-1}) - n q_k\), this becomes \[ q_k = \frac{1}{k}\operatorname{tr}(A D_{k-1}), \] and the last relation in (5) gives the same formula for \(k = n\). This is exactly the recursion (2)–(3), so \(q_k = p_k\), \(D_k = C_k\) and \(B_n = A\,C_{n-1} = p_n I\). Dividing \(A\,C_{n-1} = p_n I\) by \(p_n\) gives (4). Finally, setting \(\lambda = 0\) in (1) gives \(P(0) = \det(-A) = (-1)^n\det A = -p_n\), which is item 3 of the theorem.

The proof also shows that \(C_{n-1} = \operatorname{adj}(-A) = (-1)^{n-1}\operatorname{adj}(A)\), so the recursion delivers the adjugate even when \(A\) is singular.

NoteRemark — Connection with Newton’s Identities

Le Verrier’s original method uses the power sums \(s_k = \operatorname{tr}(A^k)\), which are the sums of the \(k\)-th powers of the eigenvalues, and Newton’s identities \[ k\,p_k = s_k - p_1 s_{k-1} - p_2 s_{k-2} - \cdots - p_{k-1} s_1 . \] For the matrix of Example 1 below, \(s_1 = 6\), \(s_2 = 14\) and \(s_3 = 36\), and the identities give \(p_1 = 6\), \(p_2 = \tfrac12(14 - 6\cdot 6) = -11\) and \(p_3 = \tfrac13(36 - 6\cdot 14 + 11\cdot 6) = 6\), the same values the matrix recursion produces.

Sign Conventions

Many texts and programs, including Mathematica’s CharacteristicPolynomial, use \(Q(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I)\) instead. Since \(A - \lambda I = -(\lambda I - A)\), \[ Q(\lambda) = (-1)^n P(\lambda). \] In Mathews’ examples the two polynomials agree when \(n\) is even (Example 2, \(n=4\)) and differ only in sign, \(P(\lambda) = -Q(\lambda)\), when \(n\) is odd (Examples 1 and 3, \(n = 3\) and \(n = 5\)). Both have the same roots.

Example 1 — A \(3\times 3\) Matrix

Use the Faddeev-Leverrier method to find the characteristic polynomial, the inverse and the eigenvalues of \[ A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 1: Start with \(B_1 = A\): \[ p_1 = \operatorname{tr}(B_1) = 2 + 2 + 2 = 6 . \]

Step 2: Subtract \(p_1 I\) and multiply by \(A\): \[ B_2 = A\,(B_1 - 6I) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} -4 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & -4 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 & -4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -6 & 1 & -3 \\ 3 & -8 & -3 \\ -1 & 1 & -8 \end{pmatrix}, \] \[ p_2 = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(B_2) = \tfrac12(-6 - 8 - 8) = \tfrac12(-22) = -11 . \]

Step 3: Subtract \(p_2 I = -11I\) and multiply by \(A\): \[ B_3 = A\,(B_2 + 11I) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 1 & -3 \\ 3 & 3 & -3 \\ -1 & 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 6 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 6 \end{pmatrix}, \] \[ p_3 = \tfrac13\operatorname{tr}(B_3) = \tfrac13(18) = 6 . \]

Characteristic polynomial: \[ P(\lambda) = \lambda^3 - 6\lambda^2 + 11\lambda - 6 . \]

Inverse: from (4) with \(p_3 = 6\), \[ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{6}\,(B_2 + 11I) = \frac16\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 1 & -3 \\ 3 & 3 & -3 \\ -1 & 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac56 & \tfrac16 & -\tfrac12 \\[2pt] \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 & -\tfrac12 \\[2pt] -\tfrac16 & \tfrac16 & \tfrac12 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Eigenvalues: the polynomial factors as \(P(\lambda) = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 3)\), so \[ \boxed{\lambda_1 = 1, \quad \lambda_2 = 2, \quad \lambda_3 = 3.} \] Also \(\det A = (-1)^{4} p_3 = 6\).

Example 2 — A \(4\times 4\) Symmetric Matrix

Find the characteristic polynomial, inverse and eigenvalues of \[ A = \begin{pmatrix} 8 & -1 & 3 & -1 \\ -1 & 6 & 2 & 0 \\ 3 & 2 & 9 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 & 7 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 1: \(B_1 = A\) and \(p_1 = 8 + 6 + 9 + 7 = 30\).

Step 2: \[ B_2 = A\,(B_1 - 30I) = \begin{pmatrix} -165 & 22 & -42 & 18 \\ 22 & -139 & -33 & 3 \\ -42 & -33 & -175 & -17 \\ 18 & 3 & -17 & -159 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad p_2 = \tfrac12(-638) = -319 . \]

Step 3: \[ B_3 = A\,(B_2 + 319I) = \begin{pmatrix} 1066 & -106 & 146 & -70 \\ -106 & 992 & 132 & -34 \\ 146 & 132 & 1087 & 67 \\ -70 & -34 & 67 & 1085 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad p_3 = \tfrac13(4230) = 1410 . \]

Step 4: \[ B_4 = A\,(B_3 - 1410I) = \begin{pmatrix} -2138 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -2138 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -2138 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & -2138 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad p_4 = \tfrac14(-8552) = -2138 . \]

Characteristic polynomial: \[ P(\lambda) = \lambda^4 - 30\lambda^3 + 319\lambda^2 - 1410\lambda + 2138 . \]

Inverse: from (4) with \(p_4 = -2138\), \[ A^{-1} = -\frac{1}{2138}\,(B_3 - 1410I) = -\frac{1}{2138}\begin{pmatrix} -344 & -106 & 146 & -70 \\ -106 & -418 & 132 & -34 \\ 146 & 132 & -323 & 67 \\ -70 & -34 & 67 & -325 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{2138}\begin{pmatrix} 344 & 106 & -146 & 70 \\ 106 & 418 & -132 & 34 \\ -146 & -132 & 323 & -67 \\ 70 & 34 & -67 & 325 \end{pmatrix}. \] Since \(2138 = 2\cdot 1069\), the entries with an even numerator reduce, for example \(344/2138 = 172/1069\); Mathews prints the matrix in that reduced form.

Eigenvalues: this quartic does not factor over the rationals, but the shift \(\lambda = \mu + \tfrac{15}{2}\) (one quarter of \(p_1\)) removes both odd powers: \[ P\!\left(\mu + \tfrac{15}{2}\right) = \mu^4 - \tfrac{37}{2}\mu^2 + \tfrac{233}{16}. \] This is a quadratic in \(\mu^2\) with roots \(\mu^2 = \tfrac{37}{4} \pm \sqrt{71}\), so \[ \lambda = \frac{15}{2} \pm \frac12\sqrt{37 \pm 4\sqrt{71}} , \] that is, \[ \boxed{\lambda_1 \approx 3.295699, \quad \lambda_2 \approx 6.592338, \quad \lambda_3 \approx 8.407662, \quad \lambda_4 \approx 11.704301.} \] All four are real, as they must be because \(A\) is symmetric. They are also positive: each row of \(A\) has a diagonal entry larger than the sum of the absolute values of its other entries, so by Gershgorin’s theorem every eigenvalue lies in \([3, 15]\). Also \(\det A = (-1)^5 p_4 = 2138\).

The matrices \(B_2\), \(B_3\), \(B_4\), the coefficients and the inverse agree with Mathews’ worked solution. Because \(n = 4\) is even, \(\det(A - \lambda I)\) is the same polynomial as \(P(\lambda)\).

Example 3 — A \(5\times 5\) Tridiagonal Matrix

Find the characteristic polynomial, inverse and eigenvalues of \[ A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. \]

The output images of Mathews’ solution to this example are missing from the archive, so every number below is our own exact computation.

Step 1: \(B_1 = A\) and \(p_1 = 10\).

Step 2: \[ B_2 = A\,(B_1 - 10I) = \begin{pmatrix} -15 & -6 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -6 & -14 & -6 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & -6 & -14 & -6 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -6 & -14 & -6 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -6 & -15 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad p_2 = \tfrac12(-72) = -36 . \]

Step 3: \[ B_3 = A\,(B_2 + 36I) = \begin{pmatrix} 36 & 10 & -4 & 1 & 0 \\ 10 & 32 & 11 & -4 & 1 \\ -4 & 11 & 32 & 11 & -4 \\ 1 & -4 & 11 & 32 & 10 \\ 0 & 1 & -4 & 10 & 36 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad p_3 = \tfrac13(168) = 56 . \]

Step 4: \[ B_4 = A\,(B_3 - 56I) = \begin{pmatrix} -30 & -4 & 3 & -2 & 1 \\ -4 & -27 & -6 & 4 & -2 \\ 3 & -6 & -26 & -6 & 3 \\ -2 & 4 & -6 & -27 & -4 \\ 1 & -2 & 3 & -4 & -30 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad p_4 = \tfrac14(-140) = -35 . \]

Step 5: \[ B_5 = A\,(B_4 + 35I) = 6I, \qquad p_5 = \tfrac15(30) = 6 . \]

Characteristic polynomial: \[ P(\lambda) = \lambda^5 - 10\lambda^4 + 36\lambda^3 - 56\lambda^2 + 35\lambda - 6 . \]

Inverse: from (4) with \(p_5 = 6\), \[ A^{-1} = \frac16\,(B_4 + 35I) = \frac16\begin{pmatrix} 5 & -4 & 3 & -2 & 1 \\ -4 & 8 & -6 & 4 & -2 \\ 3 & -6 & 9 & -6 & 3 \\ -2 & 4 & -6 & 8 & -4 \\ 1 & -2 & 3 & -4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}. \] This matches the known formula for this tridiagonal matrix, \((A^{-1})_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j}\,\min(i,j)\,\bigl(6 - \max(i,j)\bigr)/6\).

Eigenvalues: the polynomial factors as \[ P(\lambda) = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 3)(\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 1), \] so \[ \boxed{\lambda = 2 - \sqrt3 \approx 0.267949, \quad 1, \quad 2, \quad 3, \quad 2 + \sqrt3 \approx 3.732051.} \] These are the values \(2 + 2\cos\!\bigl(k\pi/6\bigr)\), \(k = 1, \ldots, 5\), given by the standard formula for the eigenvalues of this tridiagonal matrix.

\(\det(\lambda I - A)\) expanded directly \(\lambda^5 - 10\lambda^4 + 36\lambda^3 - 56\lambda^2 + 35\lambda - 6\) \(\checkmark\)
\(P(\lambda_i)\) for the five roots \(0\) exactly \(\checkmark\)
\(\sum\lambda_i = \operatorname{tr}A\) \(10\) \(\checkmark\)

Since \(n = 5\) is odd, \(\det(A - \lambda I) = -P(\lambda)\), the sign difference Mathews points out.

Practical Notes

Integer matrices

In all three examples \(A\) has integer entries, and every \(B_k\) and every \(p_k\) is an integer: the divisions by \(k\) in (3) come out exact. This holds for every integer matrix, because the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of an integer matrix are integers, and then each \(B_k = A\,C_{k-1}\) is a product of integer matrices. The method needs no fractions until the final division in (4).

Singular matrices

The recursion never divides by anything but \(k\), so it runs to completion for every square matrix, singular or not. Only (4) needs \(p_n \neq 0\). For example, \(A - I\) from Example 1 is singular (it has eigenvalue \(0\)). The recursion gives \(p_1 = 3\), \(p_2 = -2\), \(p_3 = 0\), so \(P(\lambda) = \lambda^3 - 3\lambda^2 + 2\lambda = \lambda(\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 2)\), the eigenvalues of Example 1 shifted down by one. Here \(B_3\) is the zero matrix, and \(C_2 = B_2 + 2I\) equals \(\operatorname{adj}(A - I)\), as the proof above predicts for \(n = 3\).

WarningCaution — Division by \(k\)

The divisions by \(k = 2, \ldots, n\) are harmless over the real or complex numbers. In general, the method fails in arithmetic modulo a prime \(q \le n\) (where \(k = q\) has no inverse); this is a general remark, not something the examples here exercise.

Cost

As a general operation count: the method forms \(n - 1\) products of \(n\times n\) matrices. With ordinary matrix multiplication each costs \(n^3\) multiplications, so the method costs about \(n^4\) multiplications; only the diagonal of the last product \(B_n\) is needed, which saves a little. The standard route to eigenvalues, Householder or Hessenberg reduction followed by the QR method, costs on the order of \(n^3\) operations.

Floating-point accuracy

The method is exact in exact arithmetic, and for the integer matrices above even double-precision arithmetic reproduces it exactly, since all the integers involved are small. With general real entries two separate problems appear:

  1. the coefficients \(p_k\) computed in floating point lose accuracy as \(n\) grows, and
  2. the roots of a polynomial can be extremely sensitive to its coefficients, so even correctly rounded coefficients may give poor eigenvalues.

To see both, we took symmetric matrices \(A = Q\,\operatorname{diag}(1, 2, \ldots, n)\,Q^{\mathsf T}\) with a random orthogonal \(Q\), so the eigenvalues are \(1, 2, \ldots, n\). We ran the recursion in double precision, compared the coefficients with the same recursion run at 120 significant digits, and compared the roots of the double-precision polynomial with the eigenvalues from a standard symmetric eigenvalue routine:

\(n\) largest relative error in \(p_k\) largest eigenvalue error from the roots of \(P\)
5 \(4.6\times 10^{-16}\) \(1.9\times 10^{-13}\)
10 \(2.6\times 10^{-14}\) \(3.2\times 10^{-9}\)
15 \(5.0\times 10^{-11}\) \(1.0\times 10^{-5}\)
20 \(6.1\times 10^{-9}\) \(1.5\times 10^{-1}\)
25 \(2.5\times 10^{-6}\) \(3.3\) (20 of the 25 roots complex)
30 \(8.7\times 10^{-4}\) \(7.0\) (26 of the 30 roots complex)

The eigenvalue error is the largest distance \(|r - \lambda|\) in the complex plane when the \(n\) roots \(r\) are paired one-to-one with the \(n\) eigenvalues \(\lambda\) so as to make that largest distance as small as possible. Up to \(n = 20\) every root is real, and this is simply the error after sorting both lists. At \(n = 25\) and \(n = 30\) the imaginary parts of the roots reach \(3.2\) and \(7.0\).

At \(n = 20\) the polynomial is, up to rounding, Wilkinson’s polynomial \((\lambda-1)(\lambda-2)\cdots(\lambda-20)\), and its coefficients range from \(p_1 \approx 210\) to \(|p_{18}| \approx 1.4\times 10^{19}\). To separate the sources of error, we repeated the \(n = 20\) experiment with 320 random \(Q\), also rounding the 120-digit coefficients to double precision, and finding the roots either with a standard double-precision root finder or in high precision (120 digits, so that the root finder adds no error of its own):

  • the roots of the 120-digit coefficients, found in high precision, give the eigenvalues to about \(10^{-14}\);
  • the roots of the coefficients rounded to double precision, found in high precision, are off by about \(2\times 10^{-4}\) to \(3\times 10^{-2}\);
  • the roots of the double-precision recursion’s coefficients (relative errors about \(2\times 10^{-10}\) to \(7\times 10^{-8}\)), found in high precision, are off by about \(10^{-3}\) to \(0.4\), in most cases several times more than rounding alone;
  • the double-precision root finder by itself moves the roots of the rounded coefficients by about \(0.005\) to \(0.5\).

So at \(n = 20\) both problems matter. The roots are so sensitive (problem 2) that every small error is magnified to between about \(10^{-4}\) and a few tenths: the recursion’s coefficient errors (problem 1), the rounding of the coefficients to double precision, and the root finder’s own rounding. With a double-precision root finder, the correctly rounded coefficients give eigenvalue errors of about \(0.01\) to \(0.4\), much the same as the recursion’s own coefficients (about \(0.01\) to \(0.5\)); computing the coefficients more accurately does not help unless the roots are also found in higher precision.

This is why practical eigenvalue software never forms the characteristic polynomial. For a few eigenvalues use the power method; for all of them, reduce \(A\) to Hessenberg (or tridiagonal) form with Householder transformations and apply the QR method. The Faddeev-Leverrier method remains useful for exact or symbolic computation, for small matrices, and as a neat proof that the coefficients of \(P\), the adjugate and the inverse all come from the same short recursion.
