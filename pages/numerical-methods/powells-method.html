Powell’s Method

← Numerical Methods

Source inspiration: (Mathew 2000-2019).

Description

Powell’s method looks for a local minimum of a function \(f(\mathbf{X})\) of \(n\) variables, \(\mathbf{X} = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)\), using only values of \(f\): no derivatives are needed. It is built from line searches. From a point \(\mathbf{P}\) in the direction \(\mathbf{U}\), the function \(\varphi(\gamma) = f(\mathbf{P} + \gamma\,\mathbf{U})\) depends on the single variable \(\gamma\), and can be minimised with a one-variable method such as the golden ratio search or the Fibonacci search. The simplest scheme, which Mathews calls the taxi-cab method, searches along the \(n\) coordinate directions in turn, over and over. Its path is a staircase of segments parallel to the axes, and it can be slow when the level curves of \(f\) are tilted, elongated ellipses.

Powell’s method adds two steps to each sweep. After the \(n\) line searches have taken the point \(\mathbf{P}_0\) to \(\mathbf{P}_n\), the “average direction” of the sweep, \(\mathbf{P}_n - \mathbf{P}_0\), replaces the oldest search direction, and one more line search is made along it:

\[ \mathbf{P}_k = \mathbf{P}_{k-1} + \gamma_k\,\mathbf{U}_k \quad (k = 1, \ldots, n), \qquad \mathbf{U}_n \leftarrow \mathbf{P}_n - \mathbf{P}_0, \qquad \mathbf{X}_{i} = \mathbf{P}_0 + \gamma\,\mathbf{U}_n , \]

where each \(\gamma_k\) and \(\gamma\) minimises \(f\) along its line. Instead of a zig-zag, each iteration follows a “dog-leg” path. On a quadratic function with a positive definite Hessian and exact line searches, the directions this produces are conjugate, and the minimum of a two-variable quadratic is reached exactly within two iterations (Example 3), where the taxi-cab method only creeps towards it (Example 1).

Mathews does every line search with Mathematica’s FindMinimum, started at \(\gamma = 0\). In the runs below, every line search has been recomputed in 60-digit arithmetic and taken to the local minimum of \(\varphi\) that is reached by going downhill from \(\gamma = 0\). Mathews’ printed points in Examples 1–6 differ from these by at most \(6 \times 10^{-8}\) in any coordinate.

Line Searches

NoteDefinition — Line search

Given a point \(\mathbf{P}\) and a direction \(\mathbf{U} \ne \mathbf{0}\), a line search finds a value \(\gamma^*\) that minimises the function of one variable

\[ \varphi(\gamma) = f(\mathbf{P} + \gamma\,\mathbf{U}), \]

and moves to the point \(\mathbf{P} + \gamma^*\,\mathbf{U}\). The step \(\gamma\) may be positive or negative.

If \(f\) is unimodal along the line, the golden ratio search or the Fibonacci search finds \(\gamma^*\) on a bracketing interval. When \(\varphi\) has several local minima, different line-search methods can stop at different ones, and Mathews notes that a golden ratio or Fibonacci search can give slightly different results from FindMinimum.

For a quadratic function the line search can be done exactly. If \(f(\mathbf{X}) = c + \mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{X} + \tfrac12\,\mathbf{X}^{T}\!\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{X}\) with a symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) (the Hessian of \(f\)), then \(\varphi(\gamma) = f(\mathbf{P}) + \gamma\,\nabla f(\mathbf{P})\cdot\mathbf{U} + \tfrac12\gamma^2\,\mathbf{U}^{T}\!\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{U}\) is a parabola in \(\gamma\).

ImportantExact line search on a quadratic

If \(\mathbf{U}^{T}\!\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{U} > 0\), the minimum of \(f\) along the line \(\mathbf{P} + \gamma\,\mathbf{U}\) is at

\[ \gamma^* = -\,\frac{\nabla f(\mathbf{P})\cdot\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{U}^{T}\!\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{U}}, \tag{1} \]

and at that point \(\nabla f(\mathbf{P} + \gamma^*\mathbf{U})\cdot\mathbf{U} = 0\): the gradient is perpendicular to the search direction.

Formula (1) gives every step of Examples 1, 2 and 3 as an exact fraction.

The Taxi-Cab Method

Let \(\mathbf{E}_k\) be the \(k\)-th standard base vector, with a \(1\) in position \(k\) and zeros elsewhere. Starting from \(\mathbf{X}_0\), the taxi-cab method minimises \(f\) along \(\mathbf{E}_1, \mathbf{E}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{E}_n\) in turn, which changes one coordinate at a time, and repeats the sweep.

ImportantAlgorithm — Taxi-cab method

Start with \(\mathbf{X}_0\) and set \(i = 0\).

  1. Set \(\mathbf{P}_0 = \mathbf{X}_i\).
  2. For \(k = 1, 2, \ldots, n\): find \(\gamma_k\) that minimises \(f(\mathbf{P}_{k-1} + \gamma\,\mathbf{E}_k)\) and set \(\mathbf{P}_k = \mathbf{P}_{k-1} + \gamma_k\,\mathbf{E}_k\).
  3. Set \(i = i + 1\) and \(\mathbf{X}_i = \mathbf{P}_n\).
  4. Repeat steps 1–3 for a fixed number of iterations or until the points stop changing.

Mathews’ program takes the starting point and a maximum number of iterations, and each iteration is one sweep of \(n\) line searches.

For a two-variable quadratic the effect of one sweep can be worked out in general. Write the errors from the minimum as \((e_x, e_y)\) and let the Hessian be \(\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{pmatrix}\) with \(a > 0\), \(c > 0\) and \(ac > b^2\) (positive definite). The search along \(x\) leaves \(e_x = -(b/a)\,e_y\), and the search along \(y\) that follows replaces \(e_y\) by

\[ e_y \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{b^2}{a\,c}\; e_y . \tag{2} \]

The factor \(b^2/(ac)\) is less than \(1\), so the method converges on such a quadratic, but it is close to \(1\) when the level curves are long, narrow ellipses whose axes are tilted well away from the coordinate axes.

Example 1 — The taxi-cab method on \(f(x, y) = x^2 - 4x + y^2 - y - xy\)

\[ f(x, 1.75) = x^2 - \tfrac{23}{4}x + \tfrac{21}{16}, \]

Powell’s Method

The vector \(\mathbf{P}_n - \mathbf{P}_0\) is the net move of one sweep, the “average direction” of the \(n\) line searches in it. Powell’s method searches along this direction too, and keeps it as a search direction for the next sweep, dropping the oldest one. The directions start as the standard base vectors and are stored as the columns of a matrix,

\[ \mathbf{U} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{U}_1 & \mathbf{U}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{U}_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{E}_1 & \mathbf{E}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{E}_n \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{I}. \]

ImportantAlgorithm — Powell’s method (basic form)

Start with \(\mathbf{X}_0\), set \(\mathbf{U}_k = \mathbf{E}_k\) for \(k = 1, \ldots, n\), and set \(i = 0\).

  1. Set \(\mathbf{P}_0 = \mathbf{X}_i\).
  2. For \(k = 1, 2, \ldots, n\): find \(\gamma_k\) that minimises \(f(\mathbf{P}_{k-1} + \gamma\,\mathbf{U}_k)\) and set \(\mathbf{P}_k = \mathbf{P}_{k-1} + \gamma_k\,\mathbf{U}_k\).
  3. Set \(\mathbf{U}_j = \mathbf{U}_{j+1}\) for \(j = 1, \ldots, n - 1\), and set \(\mathbf{U}_n = \mathbf{P}_n - \mathbf{P}_0\).
  4. Set \(i = i + 1\).
  5. Find \(\gamma\) that minimises \(f(\mathbf{P}_0 + \gamma\,\mathbf{U}_n)\) and set \(\mathbf{X}_i = \mathbf{P}_0 + \gamma\,\mathbf{U}_n\).
  6. Repeat steps 1–5.

Each iteration costs \(n + 1\) line searches. Mathews’ program runs a fixed number of iterations, given as its second argument. If a sweep does not move at all, then \(\mathbf{P}_n = \mathbf{P}_0\), the new direction is the zero vector and the search in step 5 is meaningless; the iteration has then converged.

Why the average direction works so well on a quadratic is explained by the next result, which holds for any quadratic with a positive definite Hessian and exact line searches.

TipTheorem — Parallel lines give a conjugate direction

Let \(f(\mathbf{X}) = c + \mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{X} + \tfrac12\,\mathbf{X}^{T}\!\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{X}\) with \(\mathbf{A}\) symmetric and positive definite. If \(\mathbf{Q}_1\) minimises \(f\) along a line with direction \(\mathbf{U}\), and \(\mathbf{Q}_2\) minimises \(f\) along a parallel line with the same direction \(\mathbf{U}\), then \(\mathbf{D} = \mathbf{Q}_2 - \mathbf{Q}_1\) is conjugate to \(\mathbf{U}\):

\[ \mathbf{U}^{T}\!\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{D} = 0 . \]

In two variables it follows that a line search from \(\mathbf{Q}_1\) along \(\mathbf{D}\) ends at the minimum of \(f\), provided \(\mathbf{D} \neq \mathbf{0}\).

Proof. At both points the gradient is perpendicular to \(\mathbf{U}\), so \(\mathbf{U}\cdot\nabla f(\mathbf{Q}_1) = \mathbf{U}\cdot\nabla f(\mathbf{Q}_2) = 0\). Since \(\nabla f(\mathbf{Q}_2) - \nabla f(\mathbf{Q}_1) = \mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{D}\), subtracting gives \(\mathbf{U}^T\!\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{D} = 0\). If \(\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{Q}_1 + \gamma^*\mathbf{D}\) minimises \(f\) along \(\mathbf{D}\), then \(\nabla f(\mathbf{X})\cdot\mathbf{D} = 0\), and \(\nabla f(\mathbf{X})\cdot\mathbf{U} = \nabla f(\mathbf{Q}_1)\cdot\mathbf{U} + \gamma^*\,\mathbf{D}^T\!\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{U} = 0\). In two variables, \(\mathbf{U}\) and \(\mathbf{D}\) are linearly independent (two nonzero vectors conjugate under a positive definite \(\mathbf{A}\) cannot be parallel), so \(\nabla f(\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{0}\). \(\square\)

In Powell’s method the point \(\mathbf{X}_1\) minimises \(f\) along \(\mathbf{U}_2\) (step 5 of the first iteration), and the second sweep ends with a search along the same \(\mathbf{U}_2\). So for \(n = 2\) the theorem applies with \(\mathbf{Q}_1 = \mathbf{X}_1\) and \(\mathbf{Q}_2 = \mathbf{P}_2\) of the second iteration, and \(\mathbf{X}_2\) is the exact minimum. As a general result for \(n\) variables, Powell (1964) showed that, with exact line searches on a quadratic with a positive definite Hessian, the directions added by the method are mutually conjugate, and the minimum is reached after \(n\) iterations, provided the direction set stays linearly independent.

Example 6 — One iteration by hand: \(f(x, y) = \cos x + \sin y\)

Use Powell’s method to find \(\mathbf{X}_1\) and \(\mathbf{X}_2\) for \(f(x, y) = \cos x + \sin y\), starting from \(\mathbf{X}_0 = (5.5,\ 2)\), where \(f(\mathbf{X}_0) = 1.617967201117\).

Step 1. \(\mathbf{U} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{P}_0 = \mathbf{X}_0 = (5.5,\ 2)\).

Step 2 (\(k = 1\)): along \(\mathbf{U}_1 = (1, 0)\),

\[ f(\mathbf{P}_0 + \gamma\,\mathbf{U}_1) = \cos(5.5 + \gamma) + \sin 2 . \]

Its derivative at \(\gamma = 0\) is \(-\sin 5.5 \approx 0.7055 > 0\), so the search goes downhill towards negative \(\gamma\), to the minimum of the cosine at \(5.5 + \gamma = \pi\):

\[ \gamma_1 = \pi - 5.5 \approx -2.358407346, \qquad \mathbf{P}_1 = (\pi,\ 2) \approx (3.141592654,\ 2), \qquad f(\mathbf{P}_1) = -1 + \sin 2 \approx -0.090702573 . \]

Step 3 (\(k = 2\)): along \(\mathbf{U}_2 = (0, 1)\),

\[ f(\mathbf{P}_1 + \gamma\,\mathbf{U}_2) = -1 + \sin(2 + \gamma), \]

whose derivative at \(\gamma = 0\) is \(\cos 2 \approx -0.4161 < 0\), so the search goes towards positive \(\gamma\), to the minimum of the sine at \(2 + \gamma = 3\pi/2\):

\[ \gamma_2 = \tfrac{3\pi}{2} - 2 \approx 2.712388980, \qquad \mathbf{P}_2 = \left(\pi,\ \tfrac{3\pi}{2}\right) \approx (3.141592654,\ 4.712388980), \qquad f(\mathbf{P}_2) = -2 . \]

Step 4 (new direction):

\[ \mathbf{U}_2 \leftarrow \mathbf{P}_2 - \mathbf{P}_0 = \left(\pi - 5.5,\ \tfrac{3\pi}{2} - 2\right) \approx (-2.358407346,\ 2.712388980), \qquad \mathbf{U} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2.358407346 \\ 1 & 2.712388980 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 5 (dog-leg search):

\[ f(\mathbf{P}_0 + \gamma\,\mathbf{U}_2) = \cos(5.5 - 2.358407346\,\gamma) + \sin(2 + 2.712388980\,\gamma). \]

This decreases all the way from \(\gamma = 0\) to \(\gamma = 1\), where it reaches \(-2\), the smallest value \(f\) can take. So \(\gamma = 1\) and

\[ \boxed{\ \mathbf{X}_1 = \left(\pi,\ \tfrac{3\pi}{2}\right) \approx (3.141592654,\ 4.712388980), \qquad f(\mathbf{X}_1) = -2\ } \]

Iteration 2. \(\mathbf{X}_1\) is already the minimum, so every line search of the second iteration returns \(\gamma = 0\), and \(\mathbf{X}_2 = \mathbf{X}_1\).

This agrees with Mathews’ module, which prints \(\gamma_1 = -2.35841\), \(\gamma = 1\) and \(\mathbf{X}_1 = (3.14159,\ 4.71239)\), and with his one-iteration program run. The textbook version of this example (Example 8.7 of Mathews and Fink, Numerical Methods Using MATLAB, 4th ed., 2004), linked from the module’s proof page, instead prints \(\gamma = 0.9816697\), \(\mathbf{X}_1 = (3.1848261,\ 4.6626615)\) and \(\mathbf{X}_2 = (3.1698887,\ 4.7197876)\). Its \(\mathbf{X}_1\) is not the minimum along its line: along the textbook’s own direction \((-2.3584042,\ 2.7123803)\) the minimum is at \(\gamma = 1.0000024\), where \(f\) is \(-2\) to ten decimals, while its printed \(\mathbf{X}_1\) has \(f = -1.997829\).

Verification: \(\nabla f = (-\sin x,\ \cos y)\) is zero at \(\left(\pi, \tfrac{3\pi}{2}\right)\), the Hessian there is \(\begin{pmatrix} -\cos\pi & 0 \\ 0 & -\sin\tfrac{3\pi}{2} \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{I}\), and \(f \ge -2\) everywhere, so \(\mathbf{X}_1\) is a global minimum.

Enhanced Powell’s Method

The basic method always discards the oldest direction \(\mathbf{U}_1\) and always accepts the average direction \(\mathbf{P}_n - \mathbf{P}_0\). Mathews points out two weaknesses. It would be better to discard the direction \(\mathbf{U}_r\) along which \(f\) decreased most, because that direction is likely to be a large component of \(\mathbf{P}_n - \mathbf{P}_0\); if it is kept, then as the iterations go on the direction set tends to become linearly dependent, a direction is lost, and the points may not converge to the minimum. And the average direction need not be a good direction in which to search. (In Example 4 the two directions held after the third iteration already make an angle of only about \(10.7^\circ\).)

The enhanced method keeps the sweep of the basic method but decides, after each sweep, whether to change the direction set at all.

ImportantAlgorithm — Enhanced Powell’s method (Mathews’ outline)
  1. Set \(\mathbf{P}_0 = \mathbf{X}_i\).
  2. For \(k = 1, \ldots, n\): find \(\gamma_k\) that minimises \(f(\mathbf{P}_{k-1} + \gamma\,\mathbf{U}_k)\) and set \(\mathbf{P}_k = \mathbf{P}_{k-1} + \gamma_k\,\mathbf{U}_k\).
  3. Let \(\Delta f_k = f(\mathbf{P}_k) - f(\mathbf{P}_{k-1})\) for \(k = 1, \ldots, n\). Find the subscript \(r\) with \(\Delta f = \lvert \Delta f_r \rvert = \max_k \lvert \Delta f_k \rvert\), the largest decrease, so \(\mathbf{U}_r\) is the direction of largest decrease in step 2.
  4. Set \(i = i + 1\).
  5. Let \(f_0 = f(\mathbf{P}_0)\), \(f_n = f(\mathbf{P}_n)\), and \(f_E = f(2\mathbf{P}_n - \mathbf{P}_0)\), the value one further step along the average direction. If \[ f_E \ge f_0 \qquad \text{or} \qquad 2\,(f_0 - 2 f_n + f_E)\,(f_0 - f_n - \Delta f)^2 \ \ge\ \Delta f\,(f_0 - f_E)^2 , \] set \(\mathbf{X}_i = \mathbf{P}_n\), keep the directions, and go to step 1. Otherwise continue.
  6. Set \(\mathbf{U}_r = \mathbf{P}_n - \mathbf{P}_0\).
  7. Find \(\gamma\) that minimises \(f(\mathbf{P}_0 + \gamma\,\mathbf{U}_r)\) and set \(\mathbf{X}_i = \mathbf{P}_0 + \gamma\,\mathbf{U}_r\).
  8. Repeat steps 1–7.

When either test in step 5 holds, the direction set is left unchanged. The first test, \(f_E \ge f_0\), says that there is no further decrease of \(f\) in the average direction. The second says that the decrease along the direction of greatest decrease, \(\mathbf{U}_r\), was not a major part of the total decrease of \(f\) in step 2. Otherwise \(\mathbf{U}_r\) is replaced by the average direction, and \(f\) is minimised along it. Stopping rules based on \(\lVert \mathbf{X}_i - \mathbf{X}_{i-1} \rVert\) or on the change in \(f(\mathbf{X}_i)\) are usually tested in steps 5 and 7. Mathews leaves the enhanced method for the reader to investigate, and his module has no worked example of it.

Implementation Notes

  • The line search decides the result. Mathews’ searches start at \(\gamma = 0\) and stop at a nearby local minimum. A search that looks over a wider bracket can stop at a different local minimum of \(\varphi\), and then Powell’s method can end in a different basin. In Example 5 the three cases differ only in the starting point.
  • Accuracy of the line search. In general, \(\varphi\) is flat near its minimum, so a line search in floating-point arithmetic locates the minimiser only to about the square root of the machine precision (about \(10^{-8}\) relative, in double precision). The differences of up to \(6 \times 10^{-8}\) between Mathews’ printed points and the exact ones are of this size.
  • Stopping. Mathews’ programs run a fixed number of iterations. A practical code stops when \(\lVert \mathbf{X}_i - \mathbf{X}_{i-1} \rVert\) or the decrease of \(f\) over an iteration falls below a tolerance, and must not search along a zero direction \(\mathbf{P}_n - \mathbf{P}_0\).
  • Direction sets. The basic method can lose a direction when the set becomes nearly linearly dependent; the enhanced method above, or periodically resetting the directions to \(\mathbf{E}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{E}_n\), guards against this.
  • Comparison with derivative methods. Powell’s method needs only values of \(f\). When the gradient and Hessian are available, Newton’s search method minimises a quadratic in one step and converges quadratically near a minimum with a positive definite Hessian, at the cost of computing those derivatives.
